# Chapter 8 - Home

At home, I broke a cup.

It was one of Mother's blue ones, not valuable but part of a set, and I had used it every morning for years without incident.

I broke it because Father came into the kitchen behind me and I jumped.

He looked at the pieces on the floor.

"What was that for?"

"You startled me."

"I walked through a door."

"I know."

Mother looked up from the table.

She did not say anything.

I swept the pieces.

The country house was louder than I remembered. Doors closed without warning. Boots crossed the porch. The pump handle struck metal. At night branches touched the roof.

None of these sounds meant anything.

I knew that.

My body did not always know it at the same time.

On the second day Mother asked me to take broth to Mrs. Turner, who had been ill.

I went gladly. It was a task I understood.

Mrs. Turner was sitting up in bed when I arrived.

"Your mother makes it too salty," she said after the first spoonful.

"I can add water."

"Then it will taste like salty water."

I laughed.

She did not.

I stopped.

"How long were you in town?" she asked.

"Several weeks."

"With those Pyncheons?"

I did not like the way she said it.

"With my cousins."

"That poor old maid and the brother."

"Hepzibah isn't..."

I stopped because she was, in fact, unmarried and older than my mother.

Mrs. Turner smiled.

"They say the brother was in prison."

The room seemed to become smaller.

"Who says?"

"Everyone knows."

"I didn't."

"Your mother didn't tell you?"

I looked at the broth.

Mrs. Turner leaned closer.

"Murder, wasn't it? Or something near it."

"No."

The word came before I had any basis for it.

"No?"

"No."

"You know better?"

I did not.

I stood.

"I should go."

"Take the broth."

"Keep it."

"It's too salty."

I took it.

On the road home I was furious with Mother.

By the time I reached the gate, I had decided she had deliberately sent me into a house where a convicted murderer lived without telling me.

I found her shelling peas.

"Why didn't you tell me about Clifford?"

Her hands stopped.

That was answer enough.

"You knew."

"I knew what people said."

"He was in prison."

"Yes."

I sat down hard.

"For what?"

Mother looked toward the door where Father had gone outside.

"He was convicted in connection with his uncle's death."

"Connection?"

"Phoebe, I don't know the case. It happened before you were born."

"Was it murder?"

"I don't know."

I hated that answer.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Would you have gone?"

I opened my mouth.

Mother waited.

"I don't know."

"Neither did I."

"That wasn't your decision."

"No."

Her agreement made me angrier.

"You let me walk into that house knowing nothing."

"Hepzibah asked that we not fill your head with old stories before you met him."

"And you agreed?"

"I did."

I stood.

"That was wrong."

Mother looked down at the peas.

"Perhaps."

I wanted her to defend herself so I could continue being angry.

She did not.

I went upstairs.

For the rest of the afternoon I thought about Clifford.

Not the Clifford I knew.

A second Clifford assembled himself from Mrs. Turner's voice, Mother's silence, and the word prison.

I remembered his hand striking mine.

I remembered him at the window.

I remembered the way he had said Jaffrey.

Things I had already seen rearranged themselves without changing.

That frightened me.

At supper I barely spoke.

Father asked whether I was sick.

"No."

Mother said nothing.

The next morning I wrote a letter to Hepzibah.

I tore it up.

I wrote to Clifford.

I tore that up too.

I began a letter to Holgrave and became angry before finishing the first sentence.

Why had he not told me?

Then I remembered telling him he had no right to decide what I should know.

That made no sense and still irritated me.

I wrote nothing.

Three days passed.

On the fourth, a letter arrived from Hepzibah.

It was short.

Clifford had asked whether the red flowers were open yet.

That was nearly all.

I read the line several times.

Mother found me packing.

"You're going back."

"Yes."

"Because of the letter?"

"Because I said I would."

She leaned against the doorframe.

"Those are not always the same thing."

I folded a dress.

"I know."

"Are you afraid of him?"

I stopped.

I could have said no.

I wanted to.

"A little," I said.

Mother nodded.

That annoyed me too.

"I'm still going."

"I know."

Before I left the next morning, Father replaced Mother's blue cup with one that did not match the set.

He put it in my place at breakfast.

No one mentioned it.
