# Chapter 2 - Holgrave

I woke early because a bird had found the roof outside my window and seemed determined to remove it one piece at a time.

The house was quiet.

I dressed without waking anyone, though I did not know who there was to wake besides Hepzibah.

Downstairs I found yesterday's pan still on the kitchen table.

I washed it.

This time I told myself I was not helping Hepzibah. I merely wanted breakfast in a pan that did not smell burnt.

That distinction satisfied me until she came in.

She looked at the clean pan.

Then at me.

"Coffee?" I asked.

"Yes."

Nothing else was said.

I counted that as improvement.

After breakfast I went into the garden.

I did not ask permission.

There were currant bushes, fruit trees, flowers, vegetables, and several hens scratching where I suspected they had been discouraged from scratching for years. One small hen looked too old to survive an argument.

I crouched to see whether she was ill.

"Careful. She is an aristocrat."

I stood.

A young man was leaning against the garden wall.

He had dark hair, workman's hands, and no hat. I disliked the fact that he had apparently been there long enough to watch me converse with a chicken.

"Does she know?" I asked.

"Constantly."

"Then that explains the expression."

He smiled.

I immediately wished I had not said anything amusing.

"You're Miss Pyncheon."

"Phoebe."

"Holgrave."

He did not offer a family name.

I waited.

He did not add one.

"You live here?"

"In the gable."

I looked up.

"Which?"

He pointed.

"Does it leak?"

"Only when it rains."

I could not tell whether he was joking.

He walked toward the hens and scattered something from his hand. They came at once.

"You feed them?"

"Sometimes."

"I thought Cousin Hepzibah did."

"Sometimes she does."

I was beginning to understand that the house contained arrangements no one had thought necessary to explain to me.

Holgrave looked toward the kitchen door.

"How is she this morning?"

"Hepzibah?"

"Yes."

"The same as yesterday."

"That could mean several things."

"Then ask her."

He looked at me.

"I usually do."

This answer irritated me, though I could not have said why.

"What do you do besides feed aristocratic hens?"

"Daguerreotypes."

He had a small case with him. He opened it and showed me a plate.

At first I saw only light. Then a face appeared when he changed the angle.

A man I did not know.

The image was so exact that I looked away and back again.

"He looks tired," I said.

"He wasn't."

"He looks it."

"That is what the plate took."

I looked closer.

"Perhaps he was tired of sitting for you."

Holgrave laughed.

"Possible."

I had expected him to argue.

He closed the case.

"People arrange themselves for paintings," he said. "A daguerreotype is less patient with arrangements."

"You think it tells the truth."

"I think it sometimes catches what people would rather smooth over."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Again he did not argue.

This annoyed me more than if he had.

He began pulling weeds near the wall.

I watched for a moment.

"Those aren't weeds."

"They are here."

"They're feverfew."

"They're everywhere."

I took one from his hand.

"My mother dries it."

"For fevers?"

"For everything, according to her."

He left the rest.

I gathered some.

For several minutes we worked without speaking.

It was easier than talking to him.

Then he said, "You won't stay."

I looked up.

"What?"

"Not long."

"Why not?"

He shrugged.

"You don't belong to this place."

I stood with dirt on my fingers.

"You've known me perhaps twelve minutes."

"Long enough to see that."

"You make pictures very quickly too, I suppose."

He looked at me then.

I had meant it as an insult.

His expression changed enough that I knew it had landed.

"Fair," he said.

I felt victorious for about three seconds.

Then he added, "Stay, then. It may be good for Hepzibah."

"I did not ask whether I should stay."

"No."

"And you don't know what is good for her."

He bent back to the weeds.

"Probably not."

There was something infuriating about a man who surrendered arguments before I had finished them.

I gathered the feverfew harder than necessary.

A window opened above us.

Both of us looked up.

I saw a hand at the curtain.

Then it disappeared.

"Who is that?" I asked.

Holgrave did not answer.

"You saw it."

"Yes."

"Who is upstairs?"

He rubbed dirt from his thumb.

"Ask Hepzibah."

"She won't tell me."

"Then I shouldn't."

I stared at him.

This was the first thing he had done that I liked without qualification.

I made sure my face did not show it.

"You enjoy secrets."

"Not particularly."

"You live in the right house for them."

He picked up his case.

"Miss Pyncheon."

"Phoebe."

"Phoebe. Don't assume every closed door here is meant to keep you out."

I thought of Hepzibah's face in the passage.

"What else would it be for?"

He seemed about to answer.

Then he shook his head.

"I have work."

He left through the garden gate.

I stood there with feverfew in my hand, angry because I believed he had been about to say something important and had decided I was too young to hear it.

That was not, I later learned, what he had been thinking at all.

But for the rest of that morning I disliked him for it.
