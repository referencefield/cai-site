# Chapter 9 - Return

The first person who told me Judge Pyncheon was dead was a boy selling newspapers.

I did not believe him.

He shouted the name as our carriage entered town. I heard Pyncheon and dead and leaned out before I thought better of it.

"Who?"

The boy ran alongside for several steps.

"Judge Pyncheon! Dead at the old house!"

"That's not true."

He grinned as if I had challenged the quality of his paper.

"Two cents."

The carriage moved ahead.

I sat back.

My hands were cold.

At the next corner two women were speaking about Clifford.

I heard his name clearly.

One saw me looking and stopped.

The other did not.

"...ran off with the sister before they found him."

I got down before the carriage reached the house.

The driver called after me about the trunk.

I left it with him.

People looked at me as I walked.

I thought this was because they knew who I was.

Perhaps they were only looking because I was walking too fast.

The shop was closed.

I knocked.

No answer.

I tried the door.

Locked.

I went around through the garden.

The red flowers were open.

I hated them for it.

The back door was locked too.

I called Hepzibah.

Then Clifford.

Then Holgrave.

Nothing.

I began to believe the women at the corner.

Not the whole thing. Only that Clifford and Hepzibah had run.

The word murder arrived afterward.

I had not heard anyone say Clifford killed the Judge. I supplied it myself.

I thought of the window.

His hand striking mine.

Jaffrey.

He mustn't.

I backed away from the door.

Someone moved inside.

I nearly ran.

The door opened.

Holgrave stood there.

He looked terrible.

"Phoebe."

"Where are they?"

"Come inside."

"Where is Clifford?"

"I don't know."

"Hepzibah?"

"I don't know."

I stayed in the garden.

"Is the Judge dead?"

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"Yes."

I could hear my breathing.

"Did Clifford kill him?"

Holgrave looked back at me.

"I don't know what happened."

That was not what he had said in my imagination on the journey home. In every version I had invented, Holgrave knew more than everyone else and disliked telling it.

"You don't know?"

"No."

"But you were here."

"Not when he died."

I stepped inside.

The passage smelled closed and stale.

"Where is he?"

Holgrave did not ask who.

He pointed toward the parlor.

I looked at the door.

"Have they taken him away?"

"Not yet."

I turned on him.

"Why not?"

"Because I found him after Clifford and Hepzibah were already gone. Because people are already talking. Because if I open that door to the whole town before I know anything, Clifford becomes the story before anyone examines the body."

"So you've left a dead man in there?"

"Yes."

"For how long?"

He told me.

I stared at him.

"That's wrong."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"I didn't know what to do."

This frightened me almost as much as the corpse.

Holgrave always knew what he thought should be done.

"We have to tell someone."

"I know."

"Now."

He nodded.

Neither of us moved.

Then there was a knock at the front of the house.

We both froze.

Another knock.

A man's voice called from the shop entrance.

Holgrave went toward it.

I caught his sleeve.

"Who is that?"

"I don't know."

"Don't open it."

He looked at my hand on his arm.

I let go.

The knocking came again.

"We can't keep the house shut," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because there's a dead judge in it."

The sentence was ugly enough to be useful.

I followed him.

At the door was a constable with another man I did not know.

Behind them stood three people pretending not to watch.

Holgrave opened the door only far enough to speak.

The constable asked questions.

Holgrave answered some.

When he said Judge Pyncheon was inside, the people behind the constable moved closer.

I went back into the passage.

I did not want to hear the next question.

The parlor door was six feet away.

I looked at it.

I had never seen a dead person.

I thought I should look at him because I was family.

Then I thought that was a ridiculous reason to look at anyone.

The door opened behind me.

I jumped.

It was the second man, the one who had come with the constable.

"Miss?"

"Yes."

"You are Miss Pyncheon?"

"Yes."

"You were here when the Judge arrived?"

"No."

"When did you last see him alive?"

I told him.

He wrote it down.

"And Clifford Pyncheon?"

My mouth went dry.

"Before I left town."

"How did he seem?"

I thought of the procession.

I thought of the prison.

I thought of the red flowers.

"I don't know," I said.

He waited.

"Was he agitated?"

"Sometimes."

"Violent?"

I looked at my hand though the mark was gone.

"No," I said.

The answer came too quickly.

The man wrote something.

I wanted to change it.

I did not know what to change it to.

That night I slept in my old room with strangers moving below.

Hepzibah and Clifford did not return.

No one knew where they were.

For the first time since I had come to the Seven Gables, I wished the house would make one of its ordinary noises.

It did not.
