# Chapter 10 - After

They took Judge Pyncheon out of the house the next morning.

I stayed in the kitchen.

There were footsteps in the passage, men's voices, a door opening, then a silence that moved with them toward the front of the house.

I washed the same cup twice.

Holgrave came in after they had gone.

"They think it was natural," he said.

I put the cup down.

"Think?"

"The physician believes so."

"What does that mean?"

"It means he found no sign of violence. He thinks the Judge died suddenly from an internal cause."

"And Clifford?"

"They still want to find him."

"So do I."

Holgrave sat at the table.

He had not shaved.

I had never seen him look untidy without seeming to have chosen it.

"I told the man Clifford wasn't violent," I said.

Holgrave looked up.

"Was he?"

"No."

I heard the speed of my own answer.

"He hit me."

Holgrave said nothing.

"At the window."

"I remember."

"I didn't tell them."

"Do you think you should have?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I."

I was beginning to hate those words.

"What if I lied?"

"Did you mean to?"

"Does that matter?"

"Sometimes."

"Not to what happened."

He rubbed his eyes.

"No."

I sat opposite him.

For a while we listened to someone moving in the shop. The constable had left a man there to prevent people from entering the house without permission.

"Did you photograph him?" I asked.

Holgrave's hand stopped.

"Yes."

I stared.

"Why?"

"Because I thought the room might be changed before anyone examined it."

"Was it?"

"Yes. People came in."

"So the picture matters."

"Maybe."

"Does it show how he died?"

"No."

I waited for him to tell me what it showed instead.

He did not.

"Can I see it?"

"Not now."

"Why?"

"Because he's dead in it."

I had not expected that answer.

I looked at the table.

"I thought you would say something about faces."

"I don't want to."

That was the end of the conversation.

By afternoon the house had become public property without belonging to anyone. People knocked. Questions arrived. A man from the newspaper tried to speak to me in the garden. I told him I knew nothing and then worried that this too was a lie.

I knew Clifford had been frightened of the Judge.

I knew he had been in prison.

I knew Hepzibah had refused the Judge entry before.

I knew Clifford and Hepzibah had disappeared on the same day the Judge died.

I did not know what those facts made when placed together.

Other people seemed less troubled by the difficulty.

Near evening, I heard the front door open and someone shout.

I ran into the passage.

Hepzibah stood there.

For a moment I did not recognize her.

Her bonnet was crooked. Her dress was stained at the hem. Her face looked gray.

Clifford stood behind her.

Alive.

I stopped several feet away.

I had imagined this moment all day.

In none of my versions was I afraid of him.

He saw me.

"Phoebe."

I could not move.

His face changed.

He knew.

Not what I had heard. Not what I had thought. Only that I was standing there when I should have run to him.

Hepzibah looked from him to me.

"Phoebe?"

I went to her first.

I put my arms around her.

She held me so tightly I could hardly breathe.

Over her shoulder I saw Clifford turn away.

That hurt more than if he had been angry.

Later, after they had eaten and the questions had begun again downstairs, I found him in the garden.

He was sitting where Holgrave had read the story of Alice.

I stood near the path.

"I'm sorry."

Clifford did not look at me.

"For what?"

There were too many answers.

"I heard things while I was away."

"Yes."

"I didn't know about the prison."

He looked at me then.

"Now you do."

"I know you were there."

"Isn't that enough?"

"I don't know."

He laughed once.

"You sound like all of them."

I wanted to tell him I believed him.

I did not know what I was promising to believe.

"Did you kill Judge Pyncheon?"

The question was out before I could soften it.

Clifford's face emptied.

I thought he would leave.

Instead he said, "No."

Only that.

I believed him.

I also knew belief was not the same thing as knowing.

I sat beside him.

He did not ask me to go.

We watched the red flowers move in the evening air.

After a while Clifford said, "I wanted to see the procession."

"I know."

"You thought I was going to jump."

"I thought you might fall."

"That's different."

"Yes."

He touched one of the flowers without picking it.

"I hit you."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"No, you don't."

I looked at him.

He was right.

"All right," I said.

We sat there until Hepzibah called us in.
