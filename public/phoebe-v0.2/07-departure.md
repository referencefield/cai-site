# Chapter 7 - Departure

The letter from home came four days later.

Mother wanted me back for a time.

No one was ill. She said this twice, which made me suspect someone had been ill and recovered before the letter was posted. There were matters at home, nothing serious, and she had not expected me to remain in town so long.

I read the letter at breakfast.

Hepzibah watched my face.

"You must go."

"She says for a time."

"Then go for a time."

Clifford pushed his cup away.

"When?"

"I don't know. Tomorrow, perhaps."

"No."

Hepzibah said, "Clifford."

He looked at me.

"You said you would bring the red flowers when they opened."

I had forgotten.

"They aren't open yet."

"Then you can't go."

I smiled.

He did not.

"I'll come back."

He looked toward the window.

"Everyone says that."

No one spoke for a while.

I wanted to ask who had said it before me.

I did not.

Packing took less time than unpacking had. I had accumulated almost nothing except a ribbon, two books, and dirt on the hem of one dress that would not brush out.

Hepzibah came into my room carrying stockings.

"These were in the wash."

"Thank you."

She put them on the bed.

I wanted to tell her I had discovered the mended one on my first night.

I wanted to apologize for the thread in the shop, the bread, the Judge, and several other things that did not belong in one apology.

Instead I said, "Will you be all right?"

Her face changed.

I had done it again.

"Of course."

"I didn't mean..."

"I know what you meant."

She folded a handkerchief that did not need folding.

"Clifford will miss you."

"I'll miss him."

"He may be difficult when you go."

"He's difficult when I stay."

Hepzibah looked at me.

Then, unexpectedly, she laughed.

It lasted only a second.

I laughed too.

That was the nearest we came to saying what we wanted to say.

I found Holgrave in the garden late in the afternoon.

He was repairing something on his camera.

"I'm leaving tomorrow."

His hands stopped.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Is someone ill?"

"No."

"Then why?"

I was offended by the question.

"Because my mother asked me to come home."

"Of course."

He returned to the camera.

"What does that mean?"

"Nothing."

"You say that when you mean something and don't want to say it."

"So do you."

I waited.

He adjusted a screw.

"Are you angry with me?" I asked.

"No."

"You haven't spoken to me properly since Alice."

"I thought you preferred that."

"I preferred you not doing whatever you were doing."

His face tightened.

"I don't know what I was doing."

"Neither do I."

"Then perhaps leaving is sensible."

I stared at him.

"You wanted me to stay before."

"You told me I had no right to decide that."

"You didn't listen then."

"I'm trying to improve."

I wanted to throw the camera into the well.

Instead I turned toward the house.

"Phoebe."

I stopped.

He came closer, but not very close.

"I am glad you came here."

I looked at him.

It was such a poor sentence that I knew he meant it.

"Even though I don't belong?"

He rubbed the back of his neck.

"I was wrong about that."

I had expected satisfaction when he admitted it.

I felt none.

"About which part?"

"I don't know yet."

I almost laughed.

Then I remembered the garden four days earlier and didn't.

"Goodbye, Holgrave."

"Goodbye, Phoebe."

He did not offer his hand.

I was glad.

Halfway to the door, I wished he had.

That night Clifford would not come downstairs for supper.

I carried a tray to his room.

He was sitting by the window.

"I brought soup."

"I don't want it."

"You should eat something."

"No."

I set the tray down.

"I'm coming back."

He did not answer.

"Clifford."

"You don't know that."

"Yes, I do."

He turned toward me.

"How?"

I opened my mouth.

I had no answer that wasn't merely a promise.

He looked away again.

I left the soup.

In the morning it was still there, untouched.
