# Chapter 12 - Leaving

The new house had a room Clifford liked before he saw any of the others.

A lawyer described it to him: two windows facing south, a view across grass, no street immediately below.

"That one," Clifford said.

The lawyer laughed.

"You haven't seen it yet."

"That one."

No one argued.

Money changed the household in practical ways first.

People came to measure things. Hepzibah was asked to sign papers and complained that no one wrote a hand meant to be read. The shop shelves were emptied. Ned Higgins received more gingerbread than was good for him because Hepzibah refused to count it.

Uncle Venner was invited to live in a cottage at the country place and spent two days pretending this was not exactly what he had always wanted.

Holgrave and I spoke when necessary.

"Is that box yours?"

"No."

"Do you know where Hepzibah wants these books?"

"Ask her."

"I did."

"Then ask again."

It was not our finest period.

I wanted him to apologize properly.

He had apologized twice.

This did not count because I remained angry.

On the morning before we were to leave, I went into the garden to cut the last of the red flowers for Clifford.

Holgrave was there.

I considered returning later.

He saw me.

"I'll go."

"It's a garden."

He waited.

"You don't have to go."

He stayed near the wall while I cut flowers.

After several minutes he said, "I didn't come to the house intending to deceive you."

"I wasn't here when you came."

"I know."

"So that is easy to say."

He nodded.

I cut another stem.

"Why did you come?"

"At first? Because of the house. Because of the Maules. Because I thought old wrongs remained alive in places like this and I wanted to see what they became."

"Like an experiment."

He looked uncomfortable.

"Something like that."

"And Hepzibah let you live here."

"Yes."

"Did she know?"

"Not then."

I put the flowers into the basket.

"That's ugly."

"Yes."

I looked at him.

No defense came.

"When did it stop being an experiment?"

He took a long time.

"I don't know."

"You never know anything now."

"I used to know too much."

I nearly smiled.

I did not want to make it easy for him.

"Was Alice's story part of it?"

"Yes."

"Were you trying to do what the Maule did?"

His face changed.

"No."

"Then what happened?"

"I don't know."

I looked away.

"I hate that answer."

"So do I."

The red flowers were nearly finished. I had cut more than Clifford needed.

"I was afraid of you afterward," I said.

Holgrave said nothing.

"Not very afraid."

Still nothing.

"You could say something."

"I'm sorry."

"You already said that."

"I still am."

I sat on the bench.

He remained standing.

"Are you still a Maule?" I asked.

"I don't think there's a cure."

I laughed before I could stop myself.

I was angry that he had made me laugh.

He smiled, then wisely stopped.

"I'm not ready to forgive you," I said.

"All right."

"And I don't know whether I trust you."

His face hurt to look at.

"All right."

"Stop saying that."

"What should I say?"

"I don't know."

This time he laughed.

I threw a flower at him.

It struck his coat and fell.

He picked it up.

Neither of us said anything about love.

Not then.

Inside, Clifford was waiting for the flowers.

"Too many," he said when I brought them.

"You asked for red ones."

"Not all of them."

"I became angry."

He looked toward the garden.

"At Holgrave?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"He talks too much."

I laughed.

Clifford chose three flowers and left the rest on the table.

Hepzibah came in carrying the old shop sign.

"Why are you bringing that?" I asked.

"I'm not."

She put it against the wall.

"Then why did you carry it upstairs?"

She frowned at it.

"I don't know."

We left it there.

The next morning the carriage came.

I walked through the shop once before we went.

Without goods, the shelves showed pale marks where jars had stood. There was dust in the corners I had never reached. One of the panes still had a smear that appeared whenever the sun struck it sideways.

I took a cloth from the kitchen.

Then I put it back.

Outside, Hepzibah was helping Clifford into the carriage though he did not need help.

He let her.

Uncle Venner would come later.

Holgrave stood near the gate.

I did not know whether he was traveling with us that morning.

No one had told me.

He held the red flower I had thrown at him the day before. It was wilted.

I looked at it.

He looked at me.

"Are you coming?" I asked.

"If you don't object."

I thought about saying that it was not my carriage.

Instead I moved my skirt to make room.

He got in.

As we turned into the street, I looked back at the shop window.

The smear caught the sun.

For a second it looked like a crack.

Then the carriage moved, and I could not see it anymore.
