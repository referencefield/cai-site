# Chapter 1 - Arrival

The driver put my trunk down before the wrong house.

I knew it was wrong because the door was blue. My mother had said Cousin Hepzibah's house had seven gables and a great elm before it, and whatever else might be said of the building in front of me, it had only one roof worth counting.

"Not here," I said.

The driver looked at the paper again.

A woman across the street had already begun watching us.

"Pyncheon?"

"Yes."

She lifted her chin toward the next corner.

We moved on.

I was annoyed to have made an entrance twice.

The Seven Gables was easier to recognize. The elm came first. Behind it the house seemed darker than the afternoon required, with roofs rising out of roofs and windows set where I would not have put them. One chimney leaned enough that I watched it while the driver lowered my trunk.

There was a shop in front.

I had known about the shop. My mother had mentioned it twice and changed the subject both times.

A little boy stood at the window with his nose near the glass. Inside were biscuits, thread, soap, small toys, and things I could not make out because the glass needed washing.

The boy looked at me.

I looked at him.

He ran away.

I smoothed my dress before I knocked.

No one answered.

I knocked again.

Something struck the floor inside. Then there was a pause long enough for me to wonder whether I ought to pick up my trunk and go back to the road.

The door opened.

Cousin Hepzibah peered at me.

I remembered her taller.

That was foolish. I had been a child the last time I saw her.

"Phoebe?"

"Yes."

She looked beyond me.

"Where is your mother?"

"At home."

"You came alone?"

"There was a driver."

The driver had already gone.

Hepzibah saw the trunk.

Her mouth tightened.

I had expected surprise, then pleasure. I had prepared myself to be useful before supper. Instead I stood on the step feeling the heat collect under my bonnet.

"I wrote," I said.

"I know."

"Mother thought I might stay a little while."

"Your mother thinks many things after she has written them."

I did not know whether this was an insult to Mother or merely family speech.

"I can go to an inn tonight if it is inconvenient."

I had no idea where an inn was.

Hepzibah looked at the trunk again.

"Don't be absurd. Bring it in."

I bent for one handle.

"Leave it."

"I can carry my end."

"Phoebe. Leave it."

I let go.

She called a boy from somewhere nearby and paid him to bring the trunk inside. I wished she had let me help. Paying for something I could do myself seemed wasteful, especially after I saw the shop.

The passage was dim after the street. I smelled old wood, coffee, and something burnt.

"Were you cooking?"

Hepzibah stopped.

"Why?"

"Nothing. I thought I smelled..."

I should have stopped sooner.

"The kitchen is this way," she said.

She walked ahead of me.

I followed.

A pan sat on the table with a blackened edge. An old book lay open beside it. Hepzibah closed the book when she saw me notice.

"I can cook," I said.

She turned.

I meant to reassure her.

Her face made clear that I had not.

"I have fed myself for a considerable number of years."

"Of course. I only meant while I'm here."

"You have not yet been here ten minutes."

I felt myself redden.

"No."

She took off her spectacles and wiped them.

Without them she looked tired rather than fierce.

I looked away before she put them back on.

She showed me a bedroom upstairs. It was clean and cold, with furniture too large for it. Someone had put fresh water in the pitcher.

That comforted me. Whatever Hepzibah thought of my arrival, she had expected the possibility of me.

"You may stay tonight," she said.

"Only tonight?"

"We will see."

I wanted to ask what there was to see.

Instead I opened my trunk.

Hepzibah stood in the doorway while I took out a dress.

"Your mother is well?"

"Yes. She says the country has made me too certain that everyone wants help."

Hepzibah made a small sound.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I could not tell whether she was agreeing with Mother.

After she left, I unpacked more slowly.

I had imagined coming into the house and finding tasks. I had imagined Hepzibah relieved by my company, though I had been careful not to picture her saying so. I had imagined sweeping, arranging the shop, making a proper supper, perhaps putting flowers somewhere gloomy.

The flowers I had brought from home were crushed at the bottom of my basket.

I put them in the pitcher anyway.

At supper Hepzibah asked questions about Mother, the farm, neighbors, weather, and a cow she remembered that had been dead six years. Whenever I asked anything about the house, she became interested in my journey.

I tried twice.

Then I gave up.

Afterward she went to the shop because a bell had rung.

I followed.

A woman wanted thread.

Hepzibah could not find the color though it was directly behind a jar of gingerbread. I reached for it.

"Here."

Hepzibah's hand caught my wrist.

Not hard.

The woman looked from her to me.

Hepzibah released me and took the thread herself.

The customer paid and left.

"I saw it," I said.

"So did I."

"Then why didn't you take it?"

She stared at me.

I knew at once that I had asked something larger than the thread.

I did not know what.

"You needn't attend the shop," she said.

"I don't mind."

"I said you needn't."

She turned away.

I went upstairs angry.

For perhaps half an hour I decided Cousin Hepzibah was proud, unreasonable, and determined to make difficulty out of assistance. I folded my clothes harder than necessary.

Then I noticed that one of my stockings had been mended.

I had not mended it.

The repair was neat and done with thread darker than the wool.

I sat on the bed holding it.

Hepzibah must have taken it from the open trunk while I was downstairs, or perhaps earlier from something Mother had sent. I could not work out when.

I was still annoyed with her.

The stocking made the annoyance less comfortable.

Later, carrying my candle along the passage, I heard voices.

One was Hepzibah's.

The other might also have been hers. I could not make out words.

I stopped.

A floorboard moved under my foot.

The voices ceased.

I waited.

Nothing.

At the far end of the passage was a closed door.

Hepzibah came around the corner below and saw me standing there.

For a moment she looked frightened.

Then her face hardened.

"Your room is the other way."

"I heard someone."

"Old houses make noises."

"It sounded like talking."

"Good night, Phoebe."

I went back to my room.

The crushed flowers had fallen sideways in the pitcher. One stem was broken completely.

I took it out and laid it on the washstand.

Then I blew out the candle.
