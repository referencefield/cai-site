# Chapter 6 - Alice

For two days after the procession, Clifford and I avoided being alone together.

This was difficult in a house where everyone seemed always to be avoiding someone else already.

The mark on my hand faded.

I was glad.

I was also annoyed by being glad.

On the third afternoon I went into the garden and found Holgrave sitting against the wall with papers across his knees.

I nearly turned back.

He saw me.

"How is your hand?"

"Fine."

"And Clifford?"

"Fine."

He looked toward the house.

"Remarkable recovery all around."

"If you're going to begin again, I'll leave."

"I'm not."

I sat on the far end of the bench.

He returned to his papers.

I waited for him to say something.

He did not.

After a while I asked, "What are you reading?"

"Something I wrote."

"That explains the silence."

He glanced at me.

I had not meant to be funny. I was still angry enough that his smile irritated me.

"It's an old Pyncheon story," he said.

"Which one?"

"Alice Pyncheon."

I knew the name. Hepzibah had once mentioned Alice's flowers in the garden and then refused to explain why they were called that.

"Was she real?"

"Yes."

"And the story?"

"Parts of it."

"Which parts?"

"That's harder."

I held out my hand.

"Let me see."

He did not give me the pages.

"It's unfinished."

"Then read it."

"You were just complaining about my voice."

"I complained about your opinions."

"Much safer."

He began.

At first I listened because I had asked him to read and did not want him to think I was bored.

Then I forgot to prove anything.

The story concerned an earlier Pyncheon, proud and wealthy, and a Maule who came into the house because the Pyncheon wanted something from him. There was a missing paper, land, family claims, and the sort of old grievance that becomes more important every time someone repeats it.

Alice entered later.

Holgrave's voice changed when she did.

Not greatly. He read more slowly.

Alice was proud too. Not in the same way as the men. She had education, beauty, confidence, and the belief that she could stand near danger without being touched by it.

I disliked that sentence in his manuscript.

"Why?" he asked.

I had interrupted aloud without meaning to.

"You already know what happens to her."

"Yes."

"So you make everything she does point toward it."

Holgrave looked at the page.

"Maybe."

"She can't even walk into a room without you making it a warning."

He crossed something out.

"What did you change?"

"Nothing important."

"Then put it back."

He laughed.

"Do you want the story or not?"

"Yes."

He continued.

The Maule in the story could influence people strangely. Whether it was trickery, suggestion, mesmerism, or something else, Holgrave did not settle. Alice allowed him to try his power because she believed he could not master her.

He did.

At least in the story.

I became aware that I had stopped moving.

A bee worked among the flowers near my shoe. Somewhere beyond the wall a cart passed.

Holgrave's voice seemed nearer than he was.

Alice obeyed the Maule. She rose when he willed it. She went where he sent her. Her pride became something he could pull against for amusement.

I wanted him to stop reading.

I did not say so.

The garden had become very still.

Holgrave lowered the page.

"Phoebe?"

I looked at him.

For a moment I could not remember what I had meant to say.

He was watching me differently.

Not as he had in the garden the first morning. Not amused.

I felt an odd heaviness behind my eyes.

"Keep reading," I said.

He did not.

"Why did you stop?"

He gathered the pages too quickly.

"Enough for today."

"I'm not tired."

"I am."

This was plainly false.

I stood.

The movement made me light-headed.

Holgrave stood too but did not come nearer.

"Did you do something?"

His face went pale.

"No."

I believed him.

Then I did not.

"You were staring at me."

"I know."

"Why?"

He looked down at the papers.

"I don't know."

That frightened me more than an explanation would have.

I walked toward the house.

"Phoebe."

I stopped but did not turn.

"I'm sorry."

"For what?"

He took too long to answer.

"I don't know exactly."

I went inside.

In the kitchen I poured water and spilled some on the table.

My hand was shaking.

I pressed both palms against the wood.

Nothing had happened.

I had listened to a story.

Holgrave had looked at me.

I had become sleepy or dizzy or foolish.

That was all I could say for certain.

When I saw him the next morning, he did not mention Alice.

Neither did I.

He spoke to Hepzibah about the weather and left without coming into the garden.

I was relieved.

By afternoon I was offended.

That evening I found the pages where he had left them on the bench.

I did not read them.

I carried them to his door and set them on the floor.

Then I knocked once and walked away before he opened it.
