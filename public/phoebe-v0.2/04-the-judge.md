# Chapter 4 - The Judge

The first time Judge Pyncheon came to the shop, Hepzibah dropped a box of needles.

She had been counting change. The bell rang, she looked up, and the box slipped from her hand.

Needles scattered across the floorboards.

The gentleman at the door laughed kindly.

"My dear Hepzibah. Have I startled you?"

I was kneeling before she answered.

"Leave them," she said.

"I'll pick them up."

"Phoebe."

Her voice made me stop.

The Judge came in.

I knew who he was before anyone introduced us. He resembled the portrait in the parlor, though the portrait had less flesh and no smile. He was a large man, well dressed, with a manner that seemed to make the little shop smaller around him.

"And this must be our country cousin."

He held out both hands.

I stood.

"Phoebe Pyncheon, sir."

"No 'sir' among cousins."

He took my hand before I had decided whether to offer it.

His face was pleasant.

I disliked being so near it.

That embarrassed me.

He bent as if to kiss my cheek.

I moved back.

Not far. Enough.

His eyebrows rose.

Hepzibah made a sound behind me.

"Forgive me," I said. "I wasn't expecting it."

"Country modesty," the Judge said.

He smiled again.

I smiled because he had.

For the next several minutes I tried to dislike him less.

He asked after my mother and remembered the name of our town. He asked whether the journey had tired me. He bought nothing but praised the shop. He told Hepzibah he was glad she had found a young companion.

Everything he said could have been said by a kind man.

Hepzibah answered him as if every word cost money.

"And Clifford?" he asked.

The shop changed.

I felt it before I understood why.

Hepzibah bent to gather the needles.

"He is resting."

"I should like to see him."

"Not today."

"Surely he would see me."

"Not today."

The Judge looked at me.

It was only a glance.

I immediately wondered whether Hepzibah was being unreasonable.

He was family. He had come politely. Clifford had so few visitors that refusing one seemed strange.

Then I remembered Clifford's face when a spoon fell.

I did not know which thought was fairer.

The Judge stepped away from the counter.

"Perhaps Phoebe might ask him."

Hepzibah stood.

"Phoebe will do no such thing."

I felt myself become hot.

Not because I wanted to ask Clifford. Because they had both used my name as if I were a piece between them.

"I can decide what I will ask," I said.

Hepzibah looked at me.

The Judge's smile widened slightly.

I regretted speaking at once.

"Of course you can," he said.

Hepzibah turned away.

I had somehow helped him.

I did not know how.

The Judge changed the subject.

That made it worse.

He asked about the country again. He told me that his own place had fine gardens and that I should visit. He spoke of fruit trees, horses, and a room with a southern exposure that he thought I would admire.

For a few minutes I forgot to be uncomfortable.

I liked gardens. I asked what varieties of pear he grew.

He knew.

We spoke about blight.

When I looked at Hepzibah, she was staring at me.

I stopped talking.

The Judge noticed.

"Your cousin and I have old disagreements," he said.

Hepzibah's mouth tightened.

"They need not trouble you."

"I didn't ask about them," I said.

"No. Sensible girl."

I disliked being called sensible by him almost as much as I had disliked being called modest.

He put on his gloves.

"Tell Clifford I came."

Hepzibah did not answer.

He looked at me instead.

"You'll tell him, won't you, Phoebe?"

I should have said that Hepzibah had already answered.

Instead I said, "If I see him."

"Thank you."

He left.

The bell gave one small ring behind him.

Hepzibah began picking up needles.

I knelt beside her.

She moved away from my hand.

"I can help."

"You've helped enough."

I sat back.

"I didn't invite him upstairs."

"No."

"I only said I could decide for myself."

"Yes."

"Was that wrong?"

She put needles into the box one at a time.

"You are not a child."

This was not an answer.

"Then tell me why you hate him."

Her hand stopped.

"I did not say I hated him."

"You don't have to."

"And you need not make judgments about things you do not understand."

I stood.

"That is convenient. Everyone in this house knows something I don't, and then becomes angry when I step on it."

Hepzibah looked up.

I expected her to scold me.

Instead she looked tired.

"Yes," she said. "It is convenient."

She carried the box into the back room.

I remained in the shop.

A needle glittered near the Judge's footprint.

I picked it up.

Later I told Clifford that Judge Pyncheon had called.

Hepzibah was not there.

I told myself this was because the Judge had asked me to tell him and there was no harm in delivering a message.

Clifford's cup struck its saucer.

"Jaffrey?"

"Yes."

He looked toward the door.

"Did he come up?"

"No. Hepzibah wouldn't let him."

Clifford stood so quickly that the chair moved backward.

"He mustn't."

"He didn't."

"He mustn't."

"Clifford, he has gone."

He was breathing badly.

I went toward him.

He raised his hand.

I stopped.

Hepzibah entered and saw us.

She looked at Clifford, then at me.

I did not need her to ask.

"I told him the Judge came."

Hepzibah closed her eyes.

Only for a moment.

Then she went to her brother.

I left the room without being told.

Downstairs, the shop bell rang.

I let it ring twice before I went in.
