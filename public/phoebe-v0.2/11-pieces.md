# Chapter 11 - Pieces

We did not learn everything at once.

The physician's opinion came first. Judge Pyncheon had died suddenly, without violence. There was talk of a weakness in the family. I heard two different names for what might have happened and understood neither.

This did not clear Clifford immediately.

Men still asked where he and Hepzibah had gone and why they had left the house.

Hepzibah answered some questions.

Clifford answered fewer.

I was asked again about the Judge's earlier visit.

This time I told them Clifford had become frightened when I mentioned it.

The man writing the answers looked at me.

"You omitted that before."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I was afraid you would think it meant something I didn't know it meant."

He wrote that down too.

I wished he had not.

Days passed.

The shop remained closed.

People stopped gathering outside, then began again whenever some new piece of the story reached town.

Holgrave was away often. He spoke to lawyers, officials, and people whose names meant nothing to me. When he returned, he sometimes had information and sometimes only mud on his boots.

I stopped asking him every time.

One afternoon Hepzibah brought out a box of papers.

"These were Clifford's," she said.

Clifford was in the garden.

"Does he want us looking at them?"

She stared at the box.

"I don't know."

We left it closed.

The next morning Clifford opened it himself.

He did not invite us.

I saw him through the doorway sorting letters into piles. Some he read. Some he tore without reading. One he held for a long time and then put into the fire.

I never learned what it said.

Later Holgrave told us that old records did not support the story of Clifford's guilt as neatly as people had once believed. There had been another will. There were questions about what young Jaffrey Pyncheon had done after the older man's death. Papers had disappeared.

"Did the Judge frame him?" I asked.

Holgrave looked tired.

"I don't know if it began that way."

"What does that mean?"

"It means a man can protect himself once, then protect the first lie, then protect everything built on it. At some point the difference between cowardice and design stops helping the person underneath."

I waited.

"Do you know that?"

He looked at me.

"No. That's what I think."

"Then say that first."

"Fair."

I almost smiled.

He did not.

The matter of the Judge's estate arrived before the old story was fully settled.

His son was expected home from abroad.

Then news came that he had died of cholera before returning.

I had never met him.

The house mourned him strangely. Hepzibah remembered him as a boy. Clifford said nothing. Holgrave had known almost nothing of him. I felt sorry for a man whose death became important to us because of what it did to property.

The lawyers explained the rest.

Clifford and Hepzibah would inherit.

So would I.

I asked them to explain why twice.

The second explanation was not better.

For several days money existed mostly as men arriving with papers.

Then Hepzibah closed the shop for good.

That I understood.

She took the sign down herself.

I offered to help.

She said no.

I let her do it.

Clifford watched from a chair near the door.

When the sign came loose, Hepzibah nearly dropped it.

He laughed.

She laughed too.

I had never heard them laugh together.

I went into the kitchen because I suddenly felt as though I were intruding.

Holgrave found me there later.

"There's something I should tell you."

I was cutting apples.

"That sentence has never improved a day."

He did not smile.

I put down the knife.

"What?"

"My family."

He sat across from me.

For once he did not begin with a theory.

"I'm descended from the Maules."

I thought I had misheard.

"What?"

"Matthew Maule's family. The people connected to the old land dispute. To Alice's story."

I stared at him.

The kitchen clock ticked.

"You knew that when you read it to me."

"Yes."

"You knew it when we met."

"Yes."

I stood.

"And you said nothing."

"No."

"Why?"

He began to answer.

I held up my hand.

"Don't tell me you didn't think it mattered."

"I did think it mattered."

That was worse.

"Did Hepzibah know?"

"Not at first."

"Clifford?"

"No."

"Was that why you lived here?"

He hesitated.

I felt something inside me go cold.

"Was it?"

"Partly. At the beginning."

I walked out of the kitchen.

He followed me as far as the passage.

"Phoebe."

"No."

"Let me explain."

"You've had months."

I went upstairs and shut my door.

For the rest of the day I reconsidered every conversation we had ever had.

This was becoming a habit I hated.

At supper Holgrave did not appear.

The next morning he was gone.

Not from the house. From town for the day, Hepzibah said.

I was relieved.

I was disappointed.

I remained angry.

Three days later Clifford asked whether anyone had looked behind the old portrait.

No one understood him.

He remembered something from childhood: a mechanism, a hiding place, perhaps only a game.

Holgrave knew where to press.

I nearly told him not to touch it simply because he knew.

The portrait shifted and came down with enough dust to make everyone cough.

Behind it was a recess.

Inside was an old parchment.

The eastern deed.

Hepzibah sat down.

Holgrave examined it.

Clifford began laughing.

"What?" I asked.

Holgrave looked up.

"It's real."

"And?"

"And almost certainly useless now."

Clifford laughed harder.

After a moment Hepzibah did too.

I did not.

I looked at the paper that people had wanted for generations and thought about all the things I had learned only after they could no longer help anyone.

Holgrave was standing on the other side of the room.

I did not look at him again.
