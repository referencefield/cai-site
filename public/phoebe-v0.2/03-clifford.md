# Chapter 3 - Clifford

Hepzibah told me his name that afternoon.

"Clifford."

She said it while looking into the cupboard.

I thought she wanted a dish.

"What?"

"My brother. His name is Clifford."

She took down a cup, put it back, and took another.

"He's here?"

"He will be."

I remembered the hand at the window.

"Was that him upstairs?"

The cup struck the shelf.

"No."

I waited.

She did not explain.

"Where has he been?"

Hepzibah turned so sharply that I wished I had asked about the weather.

"Away."

"For how long?"

"Phoebe."

I stopped.

The next morning she woke before me.

When I came down, the kitchen looked as if she had been fighting breakfast since dawn.

There were too many dishes on the table. A pot had boiled over. The old cookery book lay open. Hepzibah was cutting bread into slices so uneven that one would have fed a child and the next a farmhand.

"Let me."

She did not answer.

I reached for the knife.

"No."

"You'll cut your hand."

"I have cut bread before."

"Not evenly."

The knife stopped.

I heard my own sentence after it was too late.

Hepzibah put the knife down.

"Then perhaps you had better do everything."

She left the kitchen.

I stood there with the bread.

I wanted to call after her that I had only meant the slices.

Instead I finished cutting them.

They were very even.

This did not make me feel better.

When Hepzibah returned, she had a man with her.

Clifford.

I had expected someone weak because of the way she spoke of him. I had not expected him to look frightened by the room.

His eyes moved from the table to the window to me and back again. He was older than I had pictured, thin, with a face that might once have been handsome. He held Hepzibah's arm but did not seem aware he was holding it.

She said my name.

He looked at me.

I smiled too quickly.

His face changed.

I stopped smiling.

"I'm Phoebe," I said.

He continued looking.

Then he said, "Too bright."

I thought he meant me.

My face burned.

Hepzibah crossed the room and pulled the curtain halfway over the window.

Clifford breathed out.

I looked at the floor.

"The sun," Hepzibah said quietly.

"I know."

I had not known.

We sat down.

I poured coffee.

Clifford watched my hand.

"Do you take cream?"

No answer.

"Clifford?"

Hepzibah shook her head at me.

I put the cream beside him.

He did not touch it.

Breakfast proceeded badly.

A spoon fell. Clifford startled. Hepzibah apologized though she had not dropped it. I asked whether he wanted fruit and received no answer. I asked whether the chair was comfortable. Hepzibah told me with her eyes to stop asking questions.

I became so careful that I could hardly move naturally.

Then Clifford reached across the table and took a peach.

He held it near the window.

The color pleased him.

That was obvious even to me.

"There are more in the garden," I said.

He looked at me.

"Peaches?"

"Not yet. Flowers. Red ones. And white. I can bring some."

"Now."

Hepzibah said, "After breakfast."

Clifford's hand tightened around the peach.

"Now."

I stood.

"I'll get them."

"Phoebe," Hepzibah said.

"It will only take a moment."

I went.

When I returned with flowers, Clifford was no longer at the table.

Hepzibah stood by the window.

"Where is he?"

"Upstairs."

"What happened?"

She turned on me.

"Nothing happened. He became tired."

"I was gone two minutes."

"Yes."

There was accusation in the word.

"He asked for the flowers."

"He asks for things."

"Then why shouldn't he have them?"

Hepzibah stared at me.

"Because sometimes wanting and bearing are different matters."

I did not understand what flowers had to do with bearing anything.

I put them in water anyway.

Later Clifford came down again.

He found the flowers in the sitting room.

He touched one of the white ones.

"These."

"You wanted them."

He looked toward the door.

"She doesn't like me to want things."

I knew he meant Hepzibah.

"That's not true."

He smiled without amusement.

"You have been here three days."

I had no answer.

He turned away from me.

That was the first time Clifford made me angry.

Not because he was wrong. Because I could not tell whether he was.

During the following days I tried to learn what tired him and what pleased him.

I was good at some of it.

He liked certain flowers and disliked others. He liked music until suddenly he did not. He wanted windows open and then complained of the air. Once he asked me to read and interrupted every page. Another time I stopped reading because I thought he had fallen asleep, and he became upset that I had stopped.

"You were asleep."

"No."

"Your eyes were closed."

"Must one see a story?"

I began again.

Two pages later he told me my voice was wrong.

I put the book down.

"Then perhaps you should read it yourself."

The room went still.

I regretted it at once.

Clifford looked at the book.

His hand moved toward it and stopped.

Hepzibah, sewing nearby, did not look up.

I wanted to apologize.

Before I could, Clifford said, "I used to."

Nothing in his voice invited another question.

I picked up the book.

"Where was I?"

He pointed to the page.

I read badly for the next several minutes because I was thinking about what I had said.

That evening I found Hepzibah in the kitchen.

"I was impatient with him."

She was peeling an apple.

"Yes."

I waited for comfort.

None came.

"Does he always..."

I stopped.

"Always what?"

I could not find a kind word for difficult that did not sound worse than difficult.

"Change his mind."

"Often."

"How do you know what he needs?"

Hepzibah's knife moved under the peel.

"I don't."

That surprised me.

"But you've known him all your life."

She cut too deeply and took a piece of apple with the skin.

"Not this life."

I thought she might say more.

She did not.

The peel broke.

She began again.
