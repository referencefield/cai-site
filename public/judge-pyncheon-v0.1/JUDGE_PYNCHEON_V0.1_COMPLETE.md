# Judge Pyncheon Companion v0.1

## Chapter 1 - The Looking Glass

There are men who discover what they are by examining their hearts.

I have always preferred evidence.

A man's neighbors know whether he pays his debts. The court knows whether he keeps the law. The church knows whether he attends to his duties. His party knows whether he can be relied upon when an election grows close. A widow knows whether the treasurer of her fund has been exact. A farmer knows the quality of a bull. A horticulturist knows a pear.

These things are not sentiments. They are facts.

By such facts I had built my life.

I had held office. I had served the public. I had sat in judgment over men whose excuses were often elaborate and whose obligations were usually simple. I had contributed where contribution was proper, spoken where speech was required, kept my accounts in order, my linen white, my boots polished, my appointments punctual.

People called me honorable without being prompted.

One learns to accept the verdict of the community when it is repeated for thirty years.

There remained, of course, family difficulties.

Every respectable family has them. Ours had merely preserved its difficulties longer than most.

The old house on Pyncheon Street was one. Hepzibah was another. Clifford had been the greatest.

When word reached me that Clifford had at last returned to the Seven Gables, I received it with more agitation than I showed.

This was natural.

We had been young men together. We had suffered through the same calamity from opposite sides of it. His misfortune had affected the whole family, and no one who understood the obligations of kinship could expect me to be indifferent to his restoration.

I had, indeed, used what influence I possessed toward making that restoration possible.

There are favors which cannot be announced without ceasing to be favors. Hepzibah would never believe I had acted for Clifford's benefit. She had spent too many years arranging the world into one simple picture: Clifford injured, Hepzibah faithful, Jaffrey cruel.

A woman may live comfortably inside such a picture if no one requires her to add detail.

I did not resent her for it.

Not usually.

That morning I had business in town and took Pyncheon Street on my way.

I had not decided, before turning the corner, that I would enter the house.

But when a family matter has been neglected for thirty years, there is a certain foolishness in postponing it because the hour was not written on a card.

The shop surprised me.

Hepzibah behind a counter had once seemed an impossibility. Yet there stood the little window, the shelves, the cheap goods, all of it beneath the old family roof.

It was humiliating.

Not merely for her.

For the name.

I opened the door.

A girl stood behind the counter.

For one instant I assumed Hepzibah had hired assistance. Then I saw the resemblance around the mouth and remembered Arthur Pyncheon, my cousin and former classmate.

Phoebe.

The country child.

Not a child now.

She had his mouth, certainly, though little else that I could immediately place. There was something unguarded in her face which made the old shop look even older.

I introduced myself.

She knew my name.

Naturally.

I asked after her family. I recalled her father. I made it plain that she was not to regard me as a stranger, and, in the ordinary manner of an elder kinsman, leaned across the counter to kiss her.

She moved away.

A very small movement.

It would have meant nothing if it had not occurred before I had touched her.

My face hardened.

I knew it because I felt the muscles set before I had time to correct them.

The girl saw.

Then I smiled.

A man who has lived in public life learns that an expression, once permitted to remain, may acquire an interpretation of its own.

She apologized for being startled.

I accepted the explanation.

There was no reason not to.

Still, I studied her more carefully afterward.

She was young, inexperienced, country-bred, and therefore more likely than most people to react before deciding what reaction was appropriate.

Instinct is not evidence.

I reminded myself of that.

Then I asked about the household.

Her answer confirmed what I had heard.

Clifford was home.

The name seemed to enter the shop before the man himself did.

Phoebe spoke of him gently. Childlike, she called him. Harmless. Mild.

She knew nothing.

Perhaps that was why I envied her for a moment.

Then Hepzibah appeared.

Her welcome was precisely what I expected.

There are people whose dislike becomes an economy. They invest in it once and live upon the interest for the rest of their lives.

I offered help.

Not alms. Help.

Clifford required comfort, quiet, beauty, good food, country air. My house could provide all of these. Hepzibah could come with him. I would place every convenience at their disposal.

The offer was reasonable.

More than reasonable.

Phoebe understood that. I could see her resistance soften.

Hepzibah's did not.

She refused me access to Clifford.

I pressed only as far as dignity allowed. There was no use turning a first visit into a contest.

Time favors the person who possesses more of the world's machinery.

Hepzibah possessed an old house and a grudge.

I possessed options.

When I left, Phoebe looked troubled.

I thought again of the way she had drawn back from me.

It annoyed me because there had been no reason for it.

And things done without reason have always seemed to me the most difficult things to answer.

## Chapter 2 - The Old Matter

For many years I could pass the old house without thinking of my uncle's room.

That is not forgetfulness.

It is proportion.

A man may commit a wrong at twenty and still be something more than that wrong at sixty. If this were not so, repentance would be useless, reform absurd, and half the respected men in New England would be obliged to surrender their pews.

I had been wild when I was young.

There is no advantage in pretending otherwise now, even to myself. I spent money I had not earned. I sought pleasures which became less defensible the more frequently I sought them. I depended upon Uncle Jaffrey's favor and behaved as if his affection were an annuity that could not be revoked.

It was revoked.

Clifford had what I did not.

The old man liked him.

Clifford was handsome, delicate, pleasing, and entirely unfit for the ordinary burdens of life. Such people are often loved beyond their usefulness. They make an art of needing protection.

I despised that quality in him.

I may also have envied it.

One night I entered my uncle's private rooms.

There is no more respectable way to state this.

I had access I was not supposed to use, and I used it.

I wanted money.

Perhaps I wanted information as well. At that age the distinction was not always clear. Money was freedom, and knowledge of money was nearly the same thing.

I opened drawers.

I searched papers.

I had done enough to be ashamed before the chamber door opened.

My uncle stood there in his nightclothes.

I remember his face better than I remember what he said.

Perhaps he said my name.

Perhaps nothing.

His expression passed through astonishment into something larger. His hand rose toward his throat. He made a wet, struggling sound.

Then he fell.

His temple struck the corner of the table.

The sound was small.

I had expected catastrophe to be louder.

For several seconds I did nothing.

Then I knelt.

There was blood.

There was no meaningful breath.

The disorder that ran in our family had found him.

I had not struck him.

This mattered immediately.

It still matters.

A frightened fool would have shouted for assistance. Assistance could not have saved him. It could, however, have found me kneeling beside an opened drawer in a room I had entered for no honorable purpose.

I thought.

People have condemned me for what I did next, though most people, fortunately, never knew enough to condemn accurately.

I continued the search.

The act sounds monstrous when separated from the circumstances. In the room itself it felt like necessity. The wrong had already occurred. The old man was dead. I could not undo the search by abandoning it halfway through.

Then I found the wills.

One was older.

It favored me.

The newer instrument favored Clifford.

I read it twice.

I remember thinking that Clifford would not know what to do with the property. This was true.

I remember thinking that my uncle had been manipulated by sentiment. Possibly true.

I remember thinking that the family estate required a firmer hand. Almost certainly true.

Then I destroyed the newer will.

There are moments when a man's life divides itself and he chooses one road before understanding that there will never again be a place from which to choose the other.

I knew the act was wrong.

I did not know how much would be built upon it.

The drawers had been disturbed. Objects were missing because I had taken them. The room testified to intrusion.

If nothing were done, suspicion would search for an intruder.

I arranged what I could.

Clifford was in the house.

He had quarreled with our uncle. He was known to have expectations, sensitivities, resentments. He was the sort of man upon whom circumstances adhere because he has no force to brush them off.

I did not decide, standing beside the corpse, that Clifford should be convicted of murder.

That is important.

I intended to divert inquiry from myself.

There is a difference.

Later, the difference became less useful.

The affair darkened quickly. What had first appeared a sudden death acquired another interpretation. The blood, the disturbed room, the missing valuables, the marks of another person's presence, all of it gathered weight.

Clifford was accused.

By then I had already allowed the first false shape of events to harden.

To correct it would have required admitting everything.

Not merely the search.

The will.

The theft.

The arrangement.

My uncle's last sight of me.

People speak easily of confession when the confession belongs to someone else.

I told myself the law would distinguish what mattered.

I told myself I had not killed him.

I told myself Clifford's innocence of the death itself would emerge because innocence was a fact.

It did not.

At the trial I was careful.

I did not need to invent much.

That became, afterward, one of the facts by which I lived.

I spoke what was true and withheld what would have changed the meaning of the truth.

The distinction is not flattering.

It was legal.

Clifford went away.

I inherited.

And then a strange thing happened.

Life continued.

At first I thought of the old room every day.

Then perhaps every week.

Then only when something called it forward: a particular sound in the throat, the smell of a closed chamber, a document folded in a certain way, Clifford's name.

Years passed.

I reformed.

Truly reformed.

That fact has always angered those who prefer moral accounts to remain simple.

I became disciplined where I had been dissipated, useful where I had been wasteful, public-minded where I had been selfish. I learned the law. I served. I married. I acquired property. I assumed responsibilities. I became a man my uncle might reasonably have preferred as heir had he lived long enough to know me.

Was none of that to count because of one night?

My conscience answered this question differently at different hours.

Most days it answered as the world did.

A few minutes in a year were less cooperative.

I learned not to schedule important business during them.

## Chapter 3 - The World Agrees

Respectability is often spoken of as though it were paint.

People who have never carried responsibility imagine a man puts on a black coat, contributes to a charity, bows to his neighbors, and thereby disguises whatever he truly is.

This misunderstands the matter.

Habits become character.

A man who keeps his appointments for thirty years is punctual. A man who administers funds correctly is trustworthy in that office. A man who gives money to a cause has given the money, whatever motive a critic might invent for him afterward.

The world agreed that I had become such a man.

I agreed with the world.

My wife did not live long enough to see the full extent of my advancement. She was a nervous woman and frequently unhappy in ways I found difficult to improve by practical means. Tears answer badly to reason. I provided what was proper. When she died, I mourned her according to both affection and duty.

One of my sons became a more complicated responsibility.

He had some of the wastefulness of my own youth without enough of the force that had rescued me from it. I was severe with him, perhaps longer than mercy would have advised. By the time reconciliation came, almost no time remained in which to use it.

A father who has escaped a precipice is not obliged to smile while his child approaches the same edge.

People called the severity principled.

It was easier to remember it that way.

My surviving son took a different course and, in time, went abroad. I expected him home again. Whatever else a man accumulates, his estate finally becomes an argument made to his heirs about what he believed worth preserving.

There were offices, committees, meetings, investments, farms, improvements, elections, church affairs, charitable accounts, acquaintances to cultivate and adversaries to watch. One life can become very full if a man refuses idleness.

This fullness was useful.

It left little room for old rooms.

Clifford remained away.

Hepzibah remained in the Seven Gables, poor through a combination of pride and obstinacy that she preferred to call fidelity. I offered assistance more than once. She declined it.

Her refusals irritated me because they deprived generosity of its natural conclusion.

A gift declined is still generosity, but it leaves no visible improvement behind.

The house itself deteriorated.

I did not like seeing it.

Pyncheon Street had ceased to represent the family I believed we had become. The old place preserved too much of the century in which possession had been confused with destiny.

Yet I never quite wished to see it pass out of the name.

Contradictions of this kind are common in property. A man may dislike a thing and still understand the danger of losing it.

The old eastern claim was an example.

Nobody sensible believed that the ancient Pyncheon title to vast lands could simply be presented and converted into a principality. The world had changed. Boundaries had changed. Other titles existed. Settlements had become towns.

Still, missing property was missing property.

Uncle Jaffrey had been richer than the estate ultimately proved him to be.

This had bothered me from the beginning.

Not constantly.

But persistently.

I had known his habits. He concealed investments. He used distant arrangements. He enjoyed the power of possessing more than other men could calculate.

After his death, much of that wealth was simply absent.

Clifford had once told me something.

It was before the old man's death, in the period when Clifford still mistook delicacy for superiority and liked to taunt me with whatever he thought I could not possess.

He had spoken of a secret.

Documents.

Wealth beyond what either of us could then command.

He had enjoyed my curiosity too much for the boast to be entirely empty.

For years the memory sat beside other unresolved matters.

There was no way to question him.

Then Clifford was released.

I had worked toward that outcome.

I do not require gratitude for it. Gratitude coerced is merely another debt.

But his release changed the practical situation.

He was again within reach.

So was the old question.

I did not immediately press it.

A man who has spent years on the bench learns that timing is part of judgment.

Clifford had to settle.

Hepzibah had to exhaust her suspicion.

I had to determine what condition remained of his mind.

And I had to know whether the secret had survived where so much else in him had been destroyed.

These were not cruel questions.

They were necessary ones.

I was, by then, old enough to recognize necessity when I saw it.

Or at least when it wore a familiar face.

## Chapter 4 - A Kinsman at Large

Clifford's release created a difficulty nobody else seemed willing to name.

Freedom is not merely the opening of a door.

A man who has been shut away for thirty years may emerge with less capacity for freedom than a child. If he harms himself, frightens others, wanders, becomes violent, or is taken advantage of, who bears responsibility?

Hepzibah would say she did.

That was not sufficient.

Love is not supervision.

I therefore made inquiries.

Not spies. Inquiries.

The distinction is important because intention matters.

People notice things. A butcher delivering meat sees who answers the door. A baker hears conversation. A fish-monger learns whether a household has begun ordering for three instead of two. Customers standing at a shop counter observe who passes behind the curtain. Neighbors watch windows because windows are placed where they can be watched.

I did not create this information.

I merely allowed it to reach me.

At first what I heard was reassuring.

Clifford was quiet. He spent time in the garden. The country cousin had become useful to him. He seemed pleased by flowers, small birds, music, sunlight.

This was hardly the man I remembered.

But neither was it a dangerous man.

I told myself I was glad.

I believe I was.

Then came the account of the arched window.

There had been a procession in the street. Hundreds had seen Clifford lean outward in a state of extraordinary excitement. Thousands, depending on whose report one believed. At some point he appeared on the verge of throwing himself down among the crowd and had to be pulled back.

The story grew in transit, naturally.

I reduced it to what could be reasonably established.

He had behaved recklessly at an open upper window.

This mattered.

It also gave me something I had not possessed before: leverage that could be explained as protection.

I disliked the word leverage when it first occurred to me.

I replaced it with responsibility.

That was better and, in many respects, more accurate.

A man in Clifford's state might require guardianship. If his family refused sensible guidance, an institution existed precisely for such cases.

I did not want to commit him to an asylum.

That is true.

I wanted him to answer a question.

Those two facts were compatible.

I began revisiting the old conversation about Uncle Jaffrey's missing property.

Clifford had boasted with unusual precision. I could almost see him as he had been then: elegant, contemptuous, pleased with the power of withholding something from me.

He had known.

Or believed he knew.

The estate still troubled me because the arithmetic had never worked. Uncle Jaffrey had possessed wealth that vanished between reputation and probate.

Money does not evaporate because a man dies.

Documents may disappear.

Accounts may be concealed.

Property may be held under names that survive the owner.

Evidence leaves traces.

Clifford had spoken of those traces.

Now he was home.

I waited.

Partly because Phoebe was there.

The girl complicated the household in ways I had not anticipated. She made Hepzibah less desperate and Clifford more stable. Removing either of those effects before understanding their value would have been foolish.

I also found myself reluctant to meet Phoebe again.

That annoyed me.

Her recoil at the shop had been nothing. A young woman's startle. Country awkwardness. She had nearly warmed to me afterward when I offered hospitality.

Still, whenever I remembered the encounter, I remembered not her apology but the half-second before it.

She had moved before thinking.

People in court often did the same when a question approached something they wished hidden.

I had spent a career noticing involuntary evidence.

It was uncomfortable to become its object.

Then Phoebe left for the country.

The weather broke into a long easterly storm.

I had several affairs pressing that week, political and financial, but the change in the household removed one reason for delay.

Clifford was less supported.

Hepzibah would be more vulnerable to practical argument.

The old question had waited thirty years.

Thirty years is patience enough.

## Chapter 5 - The Storm

The horse stumbled on the road to town.

Not badly.

Enough.

An animal that has stumbled once may stumble again, and a man would be foolish to risk his neck merely because sentiment has attached itself to a favorite horse.

I decided it should be replaced.

The incident irritated me more than it deserved.

The morning already contained too much.

I had the bank directors later. A broker from State Street. An auction at which a piece of old Pyncheon land might be recovered. A political dinner. There were fruit trees to order for the country place, party funds to consider, and a physician I had half-promised myself I would consult about several trifling sensations which had lately become repetitive enough to notice.

Nothing serious.

A dimness now and then. A pressure. An unpleasant bubbling sensation in the chest once or twice.

A man becomes ridiculous when he listens too attentively to his body.

I reached Pyncheon Street in the rain.

The front entrance did not admit me.

I went through the shop.

Hepzibah appeared wrapped against the damp, looking as if the weather had been appointed for her personal use.

I asked after Clifford.

I smiled.

This was not strategy in the crude sense. Courtesy has value because it prevents unnecessary conflict. A man can be firm without abandoning civilization.

Hepzibah answered as she always did, as though every offer from me concealed a weapon.

I suggested that Clifford's isolation was unwise. I offered again to help. I asked to see him.

She refused.

Then she accused me.

Not indirectly.

She said Clifford was in no danger of death unless the same man who had persecuted him before persecuted him again.

The old matter came close enough that I felt it physically.

My first impulse was anger.

My second was control.

I spoke of duty. Conscience. Law. The grief Clifford's calamity had caused me. All true in the form in which I had carried them for years.

Hepzibah called it false.

She said I hated him.

She said I had some black purpose.

She invoked God against me with the reckless confidence of a person who possesses no earthly authority and therefore claims the highest one available.

I listened.

Something in me became very still.

There is a point in every negotiation when continued softness becomes dishonesty because it permits the other party to mistake patience for weakness.

I let the smile go.

Hepzibah saw the change and recoiled.

That reaction gave me no satisfaction.

I told her it was time to finish the discussion.

Then I gave her facts.

Clifford's release had not occurred in spite of me. I had used my influence toward it.

This was true.

I had done so for reasons more complicated than benevolence, but motives do not erase acts.

Without my representations his freedom might have been delayed indefinitely.

I had opened the door.

I could help close another.

Hepzibah stared at me.

I told her I had come to decide whether Clifford could safely remain free.

The sentence sounded harsher after I said it than it had while I formed it.

I did not withdraw it.

I had spent too much of my life watching weaker people retreat from necessary conclusions merely because a conclusion sounded unpleasant aloud.

She asked why I needed to see him.

At last we had reached the business I had actually come to settle.

The storm pressed against the shop windows.

I put both hands on the head of my cane.

The old house smelled damp.

For one instant I remembered another room, another set of drawers, another Pyncheon breathing badly.

Then the memory passed.

I began to explain the missing fortune.

## Chapter 6 - The Missing Fortune

Uncle Jaffrey had died a rich man whose estate proved smaller than his life had advertised.

This was not imagination.

Men who dealt with money had believed him immensely wealthy. He had made distant investments. He concealed holdings because concealment itself pleased him. He used arrangements that made ownership less visible without making it less real.

After his death, much of it could not be found.

I told Hepzibah this.

I also told her something she had either never known or had forgotten.

Clifford had once claimed to possess the clue.

Months before the old man's death, Clifford had spoken to me of hidden wealth with that particular mixture of mystery and superiority he enjoyed in those days. He had wanted to excite my curiosity.

He succeeded.

Even now I could recall enough of the conversation to know it had not been a random boast. There had been detail behind it. Weight.

Hepzibah laughed at the idea.

Not happily.

She genuinely believed Clifford incapable of keeping a financial secret because she believed him incapable of caring about money.

This was poor reasoning. Men often guard most fiercely what they do not know how to use.

I told her Clifford knew.

I struck the cane on the floor harder than I intended.

The sound surprised even me.

She asked what I would do if he refused to answer.

There it was.

The question I had arranged the morning around.

I explained what I had learned since Clifford's return.

His behavior had been observed. The garden. The household. The arched window.

I did not exaggerate.

I did not need to.

A man recently released after thirty years of confinement had stood at an upper window in a state of excitement and nearly thrown himself into a crowd.

No responsible relative could ignore such a thing.

If Clifford could not control himself, he might require care in a public institution.

If he refused, from hatred or irrational malice, to give information of great importance to his nearest family, that refusal might reasonably contribute to a judgment that his mind remained unsound.

Hepzibah understood the structure at once.

She called it cruelty.

I called it consequence.

She asked whether I was not already rich enough.

This annoyed me because it was irrelevant.

Enough is not a category recognized by property.

A tract of land does not cease to belong to its owner because the owner possesses another tract. A debt does not disappear because the creditor has money. Missing assets remain missing.

Furthermore, my son would inherit after me.

A man who refuses to recover family property because he can personally live without it is not generous. He is negligent toward those who come after him.

Hepzibah spoke of my age.

She spoke of death.

People invoke death when they lack a better argument against acquisition, as though mortality abolishes stewardship.

Then she spoke of the old family curse.

At that I lost patience.

A curse.

Two hundred years of bad decisions converted into supernatural bookkeeping.

I told her to speak sense.

This was business.

Clifford had information. I intended to obtain it. If he cooperated, the matter could end quietly and perhaps even benefit him. If he refused, I would act according to what responsibility required.

I gave Hepzibah the choice of summoning him herself or forcing me to proceed by harsher means.

She looked at me for a long time.

Then something in her gave way.

Not belief.

Calculation.

She understood that I had the greater power and that resisting me at the door would not remove that power.

She agreed to bring Clifford.

Before she left, she asked me to be merciful.

More merciful, she said, than my heart wanted me to be.

Then she told me God was looking at me.

I nearly answered that God had watched me for sixty years and allowed the community to form a very different judgment from hers.

I did not say it.

There are arguments one should not make aloud because their logic becomes less attractive in the hearing.

She went upstairs.

I followed as far as the parlor and sat in the old chair.

The Colonel's portrait hung above me.

For a moment I disliked the resemblance between us.

Then I dismissed the thought as one more indulgence the old house encouraged in people who spent too much time inside it.

I took out my watch.

Half an hour should be enough.

## Chapter 7 - Half an Hour

The chair was larger than I remembered.

Or perhaps I was more tired.

I sat beneath the Colonel's portrait and opened my watch.

Hepzibah had gone for Clifford.

Half an hour.

I had allowed half an hour for the whole matter, including her resistance. That had been prudent. The actual interview with Clifford should require less.

There was no need for cruelty.

I would ask directly.

He would evade.

I would explain the alternatives.

He would understand that I was prepared to act.

Then he would answer.

Weak men are often most difficult before a decision is made and easiest afterward.

I checked the watch again though almost no time had passed.

The old house was unpleasantly quiet.

Rain moved against the windows. Somewhere above, a board complained beneath Hepzibah's step.

Clifford was up there.

For thirty years I had imagined meeting him again in one form or another.

Sometimes he was still young.

Memory is lazy about faces.

Sometimes he accused me.

Sometimes he thanked me for whatever kindness imagination decided I had performed.

More often he did nothing at all. He merely stood where I could see him.

I had not expected that the real meeting would become a business appointment.

Perhaps that was better.

Business gives shape to encounters which sentiment would otherwise make disorderly.

I thought of the rest of the day.

The bank directors would expect me.

The broker would have come in from State Street regarding the loose funds I wanted placed advantageously.

There was the auction. That mattered. A portion of the old Pyncheon property, once Maule ground, might be reattached to the estate if I bid intelligently.

The horse had to be replaced.

The party committee wanted money.

Five hundred dollars would demonstrate seriousness without inviting dependency.

There was the widow's letter.

I had not decided whether to call.

There were fruit trees for the country place.

Mrs. Pyncheon's tombstone needed replacing. The sexton said it had fallen face-down and cracked.

A ridiculous inconvenience, but one does not leave such things unattended.

And the doctor.

I pressed two fingers against my chest.

Nothing.

Or almost nothing.

A sensation sometimes became more distinct simply because one looked for it. That was why physicians could make invalids of healthy men if given enough opportunity.

Fifteen years.

I had lately begun thinking in that measure.

Fifteen more would not be unreasonable.

Twenty perhaps.

My health was firm. Age had touched me less than many men younger than I. There were political honors still within reach. Property still to improve. My son would return from abroad. The estate would pass in proper order.

If Clifford's secret proved real, it would enlarge what I left him.

This was not greed.

The thought came too quickly, which irritated me.

I had not accused myself.

Why answer an accusation nobody had made?

The portrait seemed darker in the storm.

I looked up at the Colonel.

People had compared us all my life.

The resemblance was physical. Nothing more need follow from it.

Still, Hepzibah's words returned.

The curse.

Blood to drink.

I almost laughed.

The laugh did not come comfortably.

There was a pressure beneath my breastbone.

I shifted in the chair.

Too large a breakfast, perhaps.

Or the damp.

The house had always been badly ventilated.

I closed the watch in my fist.

Upstairs, Hepzibah was taking too long.

I thought I heard something and nearly called her back.

Then she appeared at the threshold.

"Did you speak?" she asked.

For an instant I was tempted to tell her to leave Clifford alone.

The thought arrived whole and without explanation.

Let the matter go.

Attend the bank.

Buy the horse.

Go home.

See the doctor.

I disliked the thought precisely because it felt like fear.

"No," I said.

My voice sounded rough.

"Why should I call you back? Time flies. Bid Clifford come to me."

She disappeared again.

I opened the watch.

The hands were perfectly clear.

For some reason the numbers were not.

## Chapter 8 - Five Minutes

There are sensations the mind rejects because accepting them would require action.

The pressure in my chest was one.

I had felt lesser versions before.

A dimness.

A momentary dizziness.

An irregular movement somewhere under the ribs.

Nothing a strong constitution could not dismiss.

This was stronger.

I drew breath.

Not enough came.

The room seemed to shift very slightly, though I knew the room was still.

I placed my right hand on the arm of the chair.

Stand.

That was the sensible thing.

Stand, open the door, call Hepzibah, ask for water, send someone for the physician.

My body did not obey immediately.

I became angry with it.

I had always disliked physical weakness because it permits no negotiation.

A debtor can be pressed. A committee persuaded. A witness examined. A political opponent outmaneuvered. Even grief can be scheduled around if a man has sufficient work.

The body simply refuses.

I tried again to rise.

Something climbed into my throat.

The old room returned.

Not this room.

The other one.

Uncle Jaffrey in his nightclothes.

His hand at his neck.

That wet, obstructed sound.

No.

The comparison was foolish.

He had been old.

I was old.

The thought followed before I could stop it.

I gripped the watch harder.

The case pressed into my palm.

Time remained measurable even if I could not read it.

The bank meeting.

The broker.

The auction.

The horse.

Five hundred dollars for the campaign.
