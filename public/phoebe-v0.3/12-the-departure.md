# Chapter 12 - The Departure

For nearly two weeks after Judge Pyncheon's death, the house belonged partly to us and partly to everyone who had an opinion about what had happened inside it.

People came with questions.

People came with explanations.

Some brought both and preferred their explanations.

The most important fact arrived from physicians: the Judge had died naturally, from the same inherited physical weakness that could also account for the death of his uncle many years before.

That did not repair Clifford's life.

Nothing could.

But it changed the old story.

Once the Judge's own death was understood, people began reconsidering what had happened decades earlier. Records, memories, and suspicions that had long been useless acquired new meaning. The account that emerged was ugly.

As a young man, the Judge had been in his uncle's rooms when the older man died suddenly. He had been searching where he had no right to search. After the death, he had continued. A newer will favored Clifford; an older one favored him. The newer will disappeared.

Then circumstances were left in such a shape that suspicion settled on Clifford.

Perhaps the Judge had not begun with a plan to destroy him.

That distinction mattered less after thirty years.

I listened to these things as they became known and felt no satisfaction.

I had imagined truth as something clean.

This truth arrived far too late to restore what had been taken.

Clifford did not want a public vindication.

I understood that more easily than I would have when I first came to the house. Society could announce that it had been mistaken. It could not give him back the years in which the mistake had been his whole world.

He needed fewer people now, not more.

Hepzibah.

Me.

Holgrave, though Clifford sometimes claimed the young man talked too much.

That seemed sufficient.

Then another piece of news crossed the ocean.

Judge Pyncheon's son had died of cholera just as he was preparing to return home.

The Judge had died believing his wealth would pass to his child.

It did not.

Instead the estate came to Clifford, Hepzibah, and me.

At first I understood this only as paper.

Men arrived. Documents appeared. Names were repeated. Figures were explained and then explained again.

Hepzibah regarded the whole process with suspicion.

Clifford seemed alternately amused and indifferent.

Holgrave, who had spent months attacking inherited wealth, became remarkably patient whenever someone explained exactly which property belonged to whom.

I mentioned this.

He said love had corrupted his principles.

I told him that sounded convenient.

Very soon we decided to leave the Seven Gables and live, at least for the present, at the Judge's country house.

No one mourned the decision.

Not exactly.

Yet on the day of departure, the old place seemed unwilling to become merely a building we had once occupied.

We gathered in the parlor with Uncle Venner while the last arrangements were made.

Holgrave had been studying the country house and began talking about architecture.

He said the Judge should have built in stone instead of wood, so that generations might alter the interior while preserving a durable exterior.

I stared at him.

"Stone?"

He looked at me innocently.

Only weeks earlier this same man had spoken as though houses should be no more permanent than birds' nests, lest one generation imprison the next inside its walls.

I reminded him.

He admitted, with surprising cheerfulness, that he appeared to be becoming conservative.

"Already?"

"Apparently."

"This is very fast work."

He looked at me in a way that made Hepzibah suddenly interested in something across the room.

Clifford, meanwhile, was staring at the old Colonel's portrait.

He said it stirred a memory.

Wealth.

A secret.

Something he had discovered as a boy and then forgotten.

Holgrave stood.

He knew what Clifford meant.

Behind the portrait was a concealed mechanism.

The machinery had rusted during its long neglect. When Holgrave pressed it, the old picture did not swing forward gracefully. It fell face-down onto the floor with a crash that sent dust everywhere.

Hepzibah cried out.

Uncle Venner laughed.

Behind the portrait was a recess.

Inside lay a folded parchment.

Holgrave opened it.

The eastern deed.

The document generations of Pyncheons had searched for.

The paper for which Gervayse Pyncheon had brought a Maule into the house.

The lost claim whose recovery had been entangled with Alice Pyncheon's ruin.

We had found it.

It was worthless.

The land had passed beyond any practical reach of the old claim long ago.

Clifford laughed first.

I joined him.

It was too perfect not to be funny: generations of ambition, fear, secrecy, and expectation ending with an authentic treasure that could purchase nothing.

Then I realized something.

Holgrave had known where to press.

"How did you know?"

He looked at me.

There was an expression on his face I recognized from every conversation in which he had decided the truth could not be postponed another minute.

"Phoebe," he said, "how would you like the name Maule?"

I stared.

Then I understood.

Not everything.

Enough.

Holgrave was descended from the Maules.

The secret of the portrait had come down through his family.

The young man who had lodged beneath the Pyncheon roof, argued against inherited institutions, photographed the Judge, told Alice's story, and fallen in love with me was carrying the other half of the house's history inside his own name.

"You might have told me sooner," I said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"I was afraid you would leave."

I considered this.

"I did leave."

"You came back."

There was no useful answer to that.

Uncle Venner wanted to know whether the parchment made anyone rich.

When Holgrave explained that it did not, he seemed satisfied. It confirmed his general opinion of distant fortunes.

Then he began talking again about the farm where he expected someday to retire.

I took his hand.

"No."

He blinked.

"No farm?"

"There is a cottage at the country place. You are coming with us."

He protested in exactly the manner of someone who hoped to lose the argument.

I described the little cottage until he stopped protesting.

The carriage arrived.

The chickens had already gone ahead to their new home, where, we were told, they had immediately begun behaving as though the improvement in family circumstances had been arranged chiefly for them.

We went through the rooms one final time.

Hepzibah lingered in the shop.

I did not speak to her.

The counter, shelves, and little window had once seemed to me evidence of humiliation. Now I thought of the courage it had taken her to open the door on that first morning and stand behind it.

Clifford waited near the entrance.

He seemed stronger than when I first saw him, though there were kinds of strength he would never recover.

That was no longer something I believed needed correcting.

Holgrave came beside me.

"Ready?"

I looked back once.

The house had not become good because we were leaving it.

Nor had it been only evil.

It had sheltered cruelty, loyalty, pride, fear, beauty, endurance, lies, ordinary breakfasts, white roses, ridiculous chickens, and two people who had discovered that they loved each other at almost the least appropriate moment imaginable.

I had arrived thinking I might brighten it.

That seemed childish now.

The house had changed me too.

I took Holgrave's hand.

Then we went out to the carriage.

Uncle Venner stood on the old porch after us for a moment, listening.

Somewhere inside, or perhaps only in his memory, he thought he heard music.

I heard nothing.

The carriage moved away.

This time, when the Seven Gables disappeared behind us, I did not feel that I had left anything unfinished.
