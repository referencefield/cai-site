# Chapter 8 - Good-Bye

I did not sleep well after Holgrave read Alice's story.

Nothing had happened to me that I could explain plainly.

That was part of the trouble.

If he had frightened me deliberately, I could have been angry. If I had merely grown sleepy, I could have been embarrassed. Instead there was a space between those explanations that I could not close.

When I saw him the next afternoon, he tried to make the matter lighter than it had felt.

I would not let him.

"You knew something was happening."

"Yes."

"And you stopped it."

"Yes."

"Could you have continued?"

He did not answer at once.

That was answer enough.

"I think so," he said.

I looked away toward Maule's well.

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because I had no right."

The words were simple.

I believed them.

I also believed the danger had been real enough for him to make the choice.

Holgrave tried to explain the tendency in himself, the strange faculty of influence he believed he possessed, and the danger of taking pleasure in power merely because one could exercise it.

I heard only part of what he said.

There were other things on my mind.

I had to go home for a little while.

When I told him, he became more serious.

"How long?"

"Not long."

"You will come back?"

"Yes."

I had meant to answer easily.

The word came out with more feeling than I expected.

He pressed my hand when we parted.

I was still annoyed with him.

That did not make me want him to release it sooner.

Two mornings later I stood ready to leave with my shawl and carpet-bag.

The house looked different because I was going away from it.

Every object I had considered ugly, inconvenient, or merely old seemed suddenly attached to a memory.

I fed the chickens and promised the venerable little hen that I would bring buckwheat when I returned.

Hepzibah watched me.

"You do not smile as you did when you came," she said.

I tried to smile more naturally and failed, which proved her point.

"It is only a little while."

"Yes."

She said it like someone who had learned not to trust little whiles.

Clifford had spoken almost nothing that morning.

Then he called me to him.

"Come closer, Phoebe."

I did.

"Closer. Let me look at you."

I leaned toward him with my hands on the arms of his chair.

He studied my face.

At first I smiled.

Then I became conscious of being studied and could not.

Something in his expression made me think of Holgrave.

The thought itself embarrassed me.

Clifford saw that too.

I felt myself blush and hated that blushing only made concealment impossible.

At last he smiled, sadly but with a tenderness that made me want to cry.

He told me that when I first came I had been a pretty young girl, but that something had changed. Girlhood had deepened into womanhood, he said.

I did not know what he had seen.

I knew only that I had not meant to show it.

"Go now," he said. "I feel lonelier than I did."

That nearly undid me.

I kissed Hepzibah, then Clifford, and went through the shop before I could begin crying properly.

Ned Higgins appeared at exactly the wrong moment.

Because my eyes were wet, I took some little object from the shop window and gave it to him without seeing clearly what it was.

He accepted it with the seriousness of a child receiving treasure.

Outside I met Uncle Venner carrying his saw-horse and saw.

He walked with me part of the way.

He said they would miss me on Sunday afternoon.

Then he said something that made leaving harder: that people could become as necessary as breathing in a surprisingly short time.

I told him I was no angel when he compared me to one.

He ignored the objection.

"Come back soon," he said.

"I will."

This time the promise felt heavier.

At the station, the train seemed impossibly modern after the old house.

Iron, steam, schedules, motion.

It carried me away with no interest in whether anyone at the Seven Gables was ready to lose me.

I watched the town disappear.

I had expected to feel relieved when I returned toward the country.

Instead I kept seeing Hepzibah's face, Clifford's searching eyes, the garden at dusk, and Holgrave's hand closing around mine at parting.

I had gone to the Seven Gables believing I might make myself useful there.

I left knowing that usefulness was no longer the reason I intended to return.
