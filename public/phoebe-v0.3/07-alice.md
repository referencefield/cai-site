# Chapter 7 - Alice

Holgrave read well.

I resented that almost immediately.

It would have been easier to keep my distance from the story if he had stumbled over it or grown pleased with his own sentences. Instead his voice settled into the garden and made the old names seem present.

He told of Gervayse Pyncheon and the long-lost eastern claim, of a Maule brought into the house because the Pyncheons believed he might recover knowledge hidden from them, and of Alice, who entered the story with the confidence of someone who had never yet discovered that another person could turn her strengths against her.

I interrupted twice.

The first time because Holgrave described Alice as proud in a way that seemed too convenient once one knew her fate.

"You already know what happens," I said. "So every quality becomes a warning."

He lowered the pages.

"You object?"

"I object to making her guilty in advance."

"I did not say guilty."

"You make the reader expect punishment."

He considered this.

Then he continued.

The second interruption came when the Maule began exercising his strange influence over Alice.

"Do you believe this?"

"Which part?"

"That he could command her."

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"I believe there are ways one mind can obtain power over another."

"That is not the same question."

"No."

I should have stopped him there.

Instead I said, "Go on."

So he did.

The story grew darker.

Whether the old Maule used mesmerism, suggestion, some inherited faculty, or only the power of Alice's own belief, the result in Holgrave's telling was the same. She yielded. He could summon her, command her, disturb her pride for amusement.

I hated him.

Not Holgrave.

The man in the story.

At least I thought that was where the feeling belonged.

Holgrave's voice changed as he read. He had given the characters gestures, tones, movements. Without noticing it, I began following those movements with my own attention.

The garden receded.

I heard the story more clearly than the birds.

My eyelids felt heavy.

The sentence on the page, the movement of Holgrave's hand, and the sound of his voice seemed to belong to one thing.

I knew I should move.

I did not.

Then he stopped.

The silence startled me.

I opened my eyes fully.

Holgrave was watching me.

His face had lost all amusement.

"Why did you stop?"

He rolled the manuscript together.

"Because the story is finished."

I knew it was not.

"No, it isn't."

"Enough of it is."

I sat upright.

The garden returned in pieces: a bee near the table, the smell of warm leaves, the dark wall, the sound of someone passing on the street outside.

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

He answered too quickly.

I stared at him.

"Do not say nothing."

Holgrave stood and moved away from me.

That frightened me more than if he had come nearer.

"You became very still," he said.

"I was listening."

"Yes."

"And?"

He hesitated.

"I think I could have made you more still."

I felt cold despite the afternoon heat.

"Like Alice?"

"I don't know how like."

"But you stopped."

"Yes."

"Why?"

His expression made the question feel foolish as soon as I asked it.

"Because you are not mine to command."

Neither of us spoke.

That answer should have comforted me completely.

It did not.

The possibility remained.

So did the fact that he had refused it.

Both mattered.

"Have you done that before?" I asked.

"Not like this."

"That is another answer I dislike."

"I know."

I stood.

My legs felt slightly uncertain.

Holgrave did not offer his arm.

I was grateful.

At the garden door I turned back.

"You should finish Alice's story sometime."

He looked surprised.

"You still want to hear it?"

"Not today."

I went inside.

For the rest of the evening I noticed every time my thoughts returned to him.

I could not decide whether that made me more afraid or less.

Two days later, I told Hepzibah that I had to go home.

It was true. There were reasons in the country that required me for a little while.

But when I said it, I thought first of the garden.
