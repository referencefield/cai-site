# Chapter 5 - The Arched Window

Clifford wanted the world in portions.

That was how I came to think of it.

The garden suited him because nothing there arrived all at once. A rose could be looked at and then left alone. The fountain could be listened to until its sound became tiresome. The little Pyncheon fowls could be admired for their absurd dignity and forgotten when they wandered behind the currant bushes.

The street was less manageable.

Sometimes I took Clifford to the great arched window above the porch. From there he could watch people pass without having to join them. The curtain kept us partly hidden, which seemed to comfort him.

At first the smallest scenes held his attention. A cart. Children on their way somewhere. A woman carrying too many parcels. An organ-grinder with a monkey that Clifford disliked immediately and with surprising force.

"Horrid little thing," he said.

I laughed, thinking he meant to be funny.

He did not.

The monkey's small imitations of human behavior disturbed him. I tried to understand why and could not.

Then one day the street filled with a political procession.

We heard it before we saw it.

Drums struck between the houses. Brass instruments answered them. The noise accumulated until the old windows trembled faintly in their frames.

Clifford came alive.

Hepzibah was with us.

Flags appeared first, then ranks of people beneath them. More followed. The separate faces mattered less as the crowd thickened. From above, the procession became one moving body.

Clifford leaned toward it.

"Look," I said, though of course he was already looking.

His hands closed around the sill.

The expression on his face frightened me because I had never seen so much longing there.

He seemed not merely to watch the people below but to feel himself being carried with them.

Then he rose.

It happened too quickly for thought.

One foot went onto the sill.

Hepzibah cried out.

I seized his clothes. She caught him too.

For one dreadful instant his whole body continued toward the opening while we pulled him back.

Then the impulse passed.

He stood trembling.

I was crying before I realized it.

"Clifford, are you crazy?" Hepzibah cried.

He drew a long breath.

"I hardly know."

His voice sounded exhausted, but there was something almost exhilarated beneath it.

He said that if he had taken the plunge and survived, perhaps it might have made another man of him.

I stared at him.

I wanted him to say he had only lost his balance.

He did not.

I wanted Hepzibah to explain what he had meant.

She could not.

That was the frightening part. Neither of us knew whether he had wanted the street, the crowd, danger, freedom, or merely one more inch of view.

I had helped stop him.

That did not mean I understood what I had stopped.

Afterward I found it difficult to return to ordinary things.

The shop bell sounded absurdly small. Ned Higgins argued over gingerbread. Someone wanted thread. Hepzibah miscounted change and had to count it again.

Clifford rested.

By the next day the procession had become something that had happened, which is the strange way even terrible moments are made to fit into life.

But I watched Clifford more carefully at windows.

I disliked myself for doing it.

On Sunday morning I prepared for church.

The weather was mild, the street bright. I put on my bonnet and went to say good-bye before leaving.

Clifford and Hepzibah were together at the arched window.

I waved from below.

Clifford smiled down at me.

For a moment he looked almost peaceful.

I went on toward church.

I did not know until later that after watching me go, Clifford had wanted to follow into the world, that he and Hepzibah had dressed themselves and actually reached the threshold before losing courage.

At the time, I knew only that when I returned they were both at home.

The fact did not strike me as remarkable.

There were many things in that house whose difficulty remained invisible until someone tried to do them.

Holgrave and I saw more of one another during those weeks.

This was partly because Clifford tired early, leaving hours of the afternoon unclaimed, and partly because Holgrave always seemed to be in the garden when I had decided I wanted solitude.

He had opinions about everything.

The house, families, property, inherited power, old names, new machines, reform, society.

I listened until I became irritated, then argued until I became interested again.

One afternoon he spoke about the Judge.

"You are still determined to dislike him," I said.

"Am I?"

"You show me his picture whenever you want to prove something."

Holgrave took out the daguerreotype.

"Look at it again."

"I have."

"Look now."

I did.

The Judge's face on the plate remained harder than the face he wore in company.

Holgrave believed this mattered.

I believed it might.

That was different.

"A picture can catch something," I said. "It cannot tell us what to call it."

"Fair."

"You say that when you do not wish to admit I am right."

"Sometimes."

I handed the plate back.

He watched me for a moment.

"You are not like the rest of them."

"The rest of whom?"

"Pyncheons."

I disliked the answer immediately.

"You know very few Pyncheons."

"Enough to recognize a pattern."

"That is what people say just before being unfair."

He smiled.

I should have been more annoyed than I was.

Perhaps that was when I first understood that arguing with Holgrave had become one of the pleasures of my day.

I did not call it pleasure then.

I called it irritation and went inside.
