# Chapter 11 - The Flower of Eden

Holgrave did not open the door.

Neither did I.

The Judge was dead in the next room. Hepzibah and Clifford were missing. Every sensible part of me insisted that the house should be opened at once and the truth brought into daylight.

Yet Holgrave looked at me as if something else had become equally urgent.

"Is it only terrible?" he asked.

"What?"

"This moment."

I stared at him.

"There is a dead man in the parlor."

"I know."

"My cousins are gone."

"I know."

"Then what else should it be?"

He tried to explain.

Before I arrived, he said, the house had seemed to him filled with death. The Judge's body had made everything around it look like evidence of guilt, punishment, inheritance, old wrongs repeating themselves forever.

Then I had crossed the threshold.

"And?"

He looked at me with such directness that I wanted to step back and did not.

"And the world changed."

I could think of no answer that did not sound foolish.

Holgrave continued before I found one.

He told me that seeing me had brought back hope, warmth, and the possibility of a future that had not seemed to exist an hour earlier.

Then he said he loved me.

There was no preparation large enough for the sentence.

For a moment I thought only of the absurdity of its timing.

Here?

Now?

With that room behind us?

I almost became angry with him for forcing something so alive into a house occupied by death.

Then the anger failed.

Because beneath my fear, and beneath everything I did not understand, I knew that this had not begun today.

It had begun in the garden, perhaps while I was accusing him of murdering feverfew with a hoe.

Or when he refused to tell me what Hepzibah had chosen to keep private.

Or when he showed me faces caught by sunlight and I refused to let him call them truth.

Or when he stopped reading Alice's story.

Or when he held my hand before I left.

I had spent my time in the country trying not to name what I carried away with me.

Now he had named his part of it.

"How can you love me?" I asked.

The question came from somewhere deeper than modesty.

Holgrave's mind ran where mine did not. He wanted to pull institutions apart simply to see whether people could live without them. He distrusted the comforts I trusted. I was happiest when things had places, when promises meant something, when food was prepared on time and people knew where they would sleep.

"You will want me to follow you into every wilderness you find," I said. "And I cannot live that way."

He listened.

That mattered.

Then he said something I had never expected from him: that perhaps I would change him too. He spoke of trees, fences, houses, permanence, and the possibility that a man who had always valued movement might someday choose to remain.

The idea alarmed me almost as much as his radicalism.

"I don't want you to become someone else because of me."

He smiled, but not lightly.

Then he asked the only question that mattered.

Did I love him?

I looked down.

It would have been possible to delay. To say this was the wrong moment. To ask for time, distance, explanation.

But those would only have postponed the answer.

"You know I do."

The words changed nothing outside us.

The Judge remained dead.

Hepzibah and Clifford remained missing.

The house remained old.

But for a few moments those facts ceased to occupy the whole world.

Holgrave took my hand.

I let him.

There was no grand revelation beyond that. No certainty about what we would become. I still feared parts of him. He still misunderstood parts of me.

Yet the uncertainty no longer meant absence.

We loved one another.

Then I heard a sound at the door.

Reality returned all at once.

"Someone is there."

Holgrave straightened.

"Then we meet the world."

We started toward the entrance.

Before we reached it, footsteps sounded from farther inside the house.

Not strong footsteps.

Slow. Exhausted.

Two voices.

I knew them.

"It is they."

I ran.

Hepzibah appeared first, so worn that for one instant she seemed to have aged years during my absence.

Clifford was with her.

"Thank God," I said.

Hepzibah saw me and began to cry.

I reached her before she could say anything.

She had carried fear and responsibility farther than her strength should have allowed. Now that she was home, and I was there, she seemed simply to stop carrying it.

Clifford looked from me to Holgrave.

His expression became unexpectedly knowing.

He spoke of Alice's flowers blooming outside and, with that strange delicacy he sometimes possessed even when everything else in him seemed confused, understood what had happened between us without being told.

I blushed.

This time I did not try very hard to hide it.

The doors still had to be opened.

The Judge's death still had to be faced.

But Hepzibah and Clifford were home.

Holgrave stood beside me.

For the first time since entering the house that afternoon, I could imagine what came after the next hour.
