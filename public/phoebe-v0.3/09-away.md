# Chapter 9 - Away

The country had not changed while I was gone.

That was the first strange thing about returning to it.

The road from the station was the same. The fields lay where they had always lain. At home, familiar chairs stood in familiar places. Mother asked about the journey before asking about the Pyncheons, which felt almost unnatural after weeks in a house where every ordinary question concealed another one.

I had expected the quiet to restore me at once.

Instead I kept hearing the Seven Gables in its absence.

A board creaked at night and I thought of the passage outside Clifford's room. Someone crossed the yard and I remembered the Judge's step in the shop. When sunlight fell across the kitchen table, I thought of Holgrave's silver plates catching a face and refusing to explain it.

Mother noticed that I was distracted.

"You wanted country air," she said.

"I have it."

"That is not the same as breathing it."

I told her she sounded like Uncle Venner.

She did not know whether that was praise.

I helped where I was needed. I cooked, carried things, visited neighbors, and returned to the rhythms I had known all my life.

The work was easier than the work at Hepzibah's because nothing in it required interpretation.

If bread burned, it was burned.

If rain came, it rained.

No one asked me to decide whether a smile was kind, a picture truthful, a silence protective, or a story dangerous.

I should have been relieved.

I missed them.

Hepzibah first.

I missed the way affection had to be discovered in her because she seemed constitutionally unable to deliver it in a convenient form.

Then Clifford.

I worried about the hours when I had usually read to him or walked in the garden with him. I could picture him asking where I was and immediately felt guilty, although I had told him exactly where I was going.

I even missed the shop.

That was proof the house had altered my judgment.

Holgrave I tried not to miss.

This required more effort than the others.

I thought about Alice's story and the moment afterward when he had possessed an opportunity to exercise power over me and refused it.

I thought about how little comfort there was in learning that someone could be trusted only after discovering what they might have done.

Then I thought about the way he had looked when I said good-bye.

Those two thoughts refused to remain separate.

I did not write to him.

I nearly did.

I told myself there was nothing to say.

That was plainly untrue, which made silence more difficult.

The days passed.

My old life did not become less dear. That surprised me too. I had half expected the Seven Gables to make everything here seem small.

Instead the country reminded me what ordinary security felt like, and that made me understand the old house more clearly.

At home, doors were merely doors.

At Hepzibah's, a closed door might mean privacy, fear, memory, shame, protection, or nothing at all.

The difference was exhausting.

It was also why I had to return.

I did not receive news of the storm that settled over the town after I left.

I did not know Judge Pyncheon had gone again to the Seven Gables.

I did not know Hepzibah had been forced to seek Clifford through those dark rooms while the Judge waited in the parlor.

I did not know the Judge had died in his chair.

I did not know Hepzibah and Clifford had fled the house in terror and confusion and taken the railway away from the scene.

Those things happened beyond the reach of my knowledge.

While they happened, I was doing ordinary things.

That fact later troubled me more than it should have.

We imagine that when disaster enters the lives of people we love, some vibration ought to reach us.

Nothing reached me.

I slept.

I woke.

I helped Mother.

And then, when the time came for me to return, I packed my trunk and traveled back toward town believing I was going to find the same people waiting in the same house.

I brought the buckwheat I had promised the old chicken.
