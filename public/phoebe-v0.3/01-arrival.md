# Chapter 1 - The Door Opens

I arrived at the House of the Seven Gables at the end of a day that seemed already tired of itself.

I had expected Cousin Hepzibah to know I was coming.

Mother had written.

That fact had seemed sufficient when I left home.

It became less sufficient when Hepzibah opened the door and looked at me as though I were a parcel delivered to the wrong family.

"Phoebe?"

"Yes."

Her eyes moved past me, perhaps expecting Mother, perhaps hoping for an explanation standing on the pavement.

There was only my trunk.

"You received the letter?"

She did not answer at once.

I learned later that the letter had been delayed in the pocket of the penny-postman. At the time I knew only that my cousin had not expected me, and that I had arrived with the confidence of someone who had mistaken intention for arrangement.

"Come in," she said.

Her voice did not make the invitation warmer, but the door opened farther.

That was enough.

The house was darker inside than the street. It smelled of old wood and enclosed rooms, with something sweet underneath that I could not place. My first thought was that the windows needed opening.

My second was that I had better not say so.

I followed Hepzibah through the passage.

There was a shop at the front of the house. I had known that from Mother's letter, though the idea of a Pyncheon keeping a cent-shop had been communicated in the family with the careful vagueness people use when they wish not to sound ashamed.

The shelves held small things: thread, flour, sugar, gingerbread, toys, soap, needles, and enough other goods to make the room look busier than Hepzibah did.

She saw me looking.

"It is mine," she said.

"I know."

"Your mother told you?"

"Yes."

"And what did she say?"

This was a dangerous question.

"That you had opened a shop."

Hepzibah gave me a look that told me Mother had been wiser than I had appreciated.

"Your room is upstairs."

I carried what I could and allowed the rest to be brought after me.

The chamber looked into the garden. The bed had curtains heavy enough to turn one corner of the room into night even before sunset. The furniture seemed to have been designed for larger people in larger clothes who expected never to move it.

I put my bonnet down.

Hepzibah remained by the door.

"You may stay tonight."

I turned.

"Only tonight?"

"We shall see."

It was not what I had imagined.

At home, the visit had been spoken of as settled. I would come, I would help Cousin Hepzibah, I would see something of town life, and perhaps stay until I was wanted again in the country.

No one had included the possibility that Hepzibah might not want help.

"I don't mean to inconvenience you," I said.

Her expression changed, and I knew immediately that I had made things worse.

"I have managed my own inconveniences for a great many years."

"I didn't mean yours. I meant me."

That was worse still.

I stopped.

For a moment we stood looking at each other.

Then Hepzibah sighed.

"Unpack, child."

She left.

I sat on the bed and considered going home the next morning simply to avoid having to become better acquainted with my own embarrassment.

Then I looked out the window.

The garden below was old but not dead. A rosebush climbed against the house, heavy with white flowers. Beyond it I could see currant bushes, a little arbor, vegetables, and something moving near a wooden coop.

The house had made me feel as though I had entered a room after an argument.

The garden did not.

The next morning I woke early.

For a few seconds I forgot where I was. Then the dark curtains, the high-backed chair, and the unfamiliar ceiling returned the whole matter to me at once.

I got up, dressed, said my prayers, and opened the window.

The air smelled damp and green.

From above, the white roses looked perfect.

When I went down and saw them more closely, I found mildew in some of their hearts.

I liked them better for that.

I gathered the soundest blossoms and carried them upstairs. By the time I had arranged the room, made the bed, put my clothes away, and set the roses where the morning light could reach them, it looked less like a chamber that had survived several generations and more like somewhere a person might live.

That was the sort of work I understood.

Downstairs, the kitchen offered further opportunities.

Hepzibah found me there.

I had moved two things.

Only two.

She noticed both.

"I was making room," I said.

"For what?"

"Breakfast."

"There was room for breakfast yesterday."

I looked at the table.

"There will be more today."

Her mouth tightened.

I thought I had offended her again.

Then, unexpectedly, she sat down.

"Do as you like."

It did not sound like permission.

I took it anyway.

Within an hour the coffee was better, the fire steadier, and Hepzibah had eaten more than she seemed to realize.

When the shop bell rang, she rose with visible reluctance.

I followed.

"You needn't," she said.

"I know."

That answer seemed to leave her nowhere to go.

The first customer wanted thread.

Hepzibah searched the wrong shelf.

I saw the color immediately, but remembered the look she had given me the previous night.

I kept my hands folded.

She searched longer.

The customer shifted her weight.

At last Hepzibah found it.

When the woman left, my cousin turned to me.

"You saw it."

"Yes."

"Why did you not say so?"

I stared at her.

"I thought you did not want help."

"I did not say that."

I almost laughed, but her face stopped me.

"No," I said. "You didn't."

Something changed between us then, not enough to call comfort, but enough to make the room easier to stand in.

The bell rang again.

Hepzibah looked at me.

I went to the counter.

That was how I stayed.
