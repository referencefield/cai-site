# Chapter 2 - The Garden

By the second morning I had discovered that the garden belonged to everyone and no one.

Hepzibah used it, but did not seem to tend it except in the way one tends an old relative: by allowing its habits to continue undisturbed.

There were vegetables, currants, flowers, the old white rosebush, and a family of chickens so small and solemn that they looked like reduced copies of ordinary fowls.

I brought scraps from the kitchen.

One of the little birds came near my shoe. I crouched and called softly until it gathered courage enough to peck at the crumbs.

Then it fluttered onto my shoulder.

"That little fowl pays you a high compliment."

I turned so quickly that the chicken nearly lost its footing.

A young man stood behind me holding a hoe.

He was not dressed like a gentleman who had come calling, nor like a servant who expected instructions. He looked altogether too much at home for someone I did not know.

"Does it?" I asked.

"They have known me much longer and never shown such confidence."

He glanced toward the coop.

"Miss Hepzibah will probably say they recognize the family blood."

I put the chicken down.

"Or perhaps I know how to speak to chickens."

He smiled.

"That would be more useful."

I knew who he must be.

Hepzibah had mentioned the daguerreotypist who lodged in one of the gables with the kind of disapproval that made further questions irresistible.

"You're Mr. Holgrave."

"And you're Phoebe Pyncheon."

I did not like that he already knew my name.

Then I remembered that I had arrived in a house where everyone seemed to know something before telling me.

"Do you garden?"

"Badly. It clears the head."

"You are digging very near the tomatoes."

He looked down.

"Am I?"

"Yes."

He moved the hoe.

I liked him slightly better for not defending himself.

He told me he made daguerreotypes, pictures made from light. I had seen them before, but never heard anyone describe the work as if sunlight were a material he handled with his fingers.

Later he showed me one.

A face appeared on the plate when he tilted it.

I leaned closer.

"It changes when you move it."

"The plate doesn't. You do."

I looked at him.

"That sounds like something you have been waiting to say."

"Possibly."

He showed me another portrait and began talking about faces, about how people arranged themselves before painters and how the sun had less patience with pretense.

I listened, though I did not accept the conclusion merely because he delivered it confidently.

"The sun can show a wrinkle," I said. "It cannot tell you why it is there."

Holgrave looked pleased.

"No."

"Then it does not tell the whole truth."

"Nothing does."

That answer stayed with me longer than I wanted.

He asked how I liked the house.

I said I had been there too little time to know.

He said most people decided sooner.

"Do you?"

"About houses, often. About people, regrettably."

"And have you decided about me?"

"Not yet."

I did not believe him.

He had the manner of someone who was always forming conclusions and then revising them for sport.

We talked until the shop bell rang inside.

I stood.

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"You are helping her?"

"Yes."

"She lets you?"

"Sometimes."

He laughed quietly.

I went inside before I asked what he found amusing.

The shop became easier after that.

I learned which customers expected conversation and which wished only to purchase something and escape Hepzibah's scowl. Ned Higgins came for gingerbread often enough that I suspected his appetite was partly curiosity. Uncle Venner stopped to talk and offered advice that was sometimes useful and always free.

Hepzibah complained that I gave people too much.

Then she gave them more herself.

The house changed in small ways.

I moved flowers into rooms that had forgotten flowers existed. I opened windows when the weather allowed. I learned that dust returns even when one takes its removal personally.

But there were limits to what brightness could do.

There was still the closed part of the house.

There was still the name Hepzibah sometimes almost spoke and then did not.

And there was the way her whole attention would suddenly turn toward a sound upstairs.

One afternoon I asked Holgrave directly.

"Who is Clifford?"

He stopped working.

That alone told me the question mattered.

"Hepzibah should tell you."

"She won't."

"Then I shouldn't."

I was irritated.

"Is silence a rule here?"

"No. It is more of a family habit."

"You are not family."

His expression changed by so little that I might have imagined it.

"No," he said.

I expected him to add something.

He did not.

That evening, Hepzibah told me Clifford was her brother and that he was coming home.

Nothing in the sentence explained why she gripped the back of the chair while she said it.

"When?"

"Soon."

"Where has he been?"

She looked at me.

"Away."

I knew enough by then not to push immediately.

But after she left, I went to my room and stood by the window looking down at the garden.

Holgrave was there alone, finishing some task in the fading light.

He glanced up once.

I stepped back before I knew why.

For the first time since arriving, the house did not seem merely old.

It seemed to be waiting.
