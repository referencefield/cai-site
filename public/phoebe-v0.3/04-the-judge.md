# Chapter 4 - The Smile

Judge Pyncheon entered the shop as if he had every right to be welcomed there.

Perhaps he did.

He was family.

That was part of the difficulty.

I knew him first by resemblance. The old portrait in the house had already taught me the Pyncheon face in its sternest form, and the Judge seemed for one instant like that ancestor brought forward into modern clothes and increased in size.

Then he smiled.

The resemblance became less simple.

"And this is our little country cousin!"

His voice was warm. His clothes were excellent. His manner suggested that everybody in the room had been waiting for him to improve the atmosphere.

Hepzibah did not look improved.

She stood behind the counter with one hand flat against the wood.

The Judge came toward me.

"Phoebe Pyncheon?"

"Yes, sir."

"No ceremony between cousins."

He opened his arms slightly, the gesture broad and affectionate, and bent toward me as if a kiss were the most natural conclusion in the world.

I moved back.

I did not decide to.

My body did it first.

He stopped.

I immediately wished I could take the movement back without stepping toward him.

"I beg your pardon," I said. "I was startled."

The Judge laughed.

"Quite right! A young lady cannot be too careful."

His good humor should have relieved me.

It did not.

I tried to correct myself. He asked after Mother. He remembered details about our family. He spoke kindly of Hepzibah's shop and said she had shown admirable courage in undertaking it.

Every sentence was proper.

That made my discomfort embarrassing.

I began looking for reasons to justify it and distrusted myself for doing so.

Then he asked for Clifford.

The room changed before anyone moved.

"He is not receiving visitors," Hepzibah said.

"My dear cousin, I am hardly a visitor."

"Not today."

The Judge's smile remained.

"Surely Clifford bears me no ill will."

Hepzibah said nothing.

He looked at me.

Only for a moment.

It was enough to make me feel that I had somehow become another door through which he might enter.

"Perhaps Phoebe will carry him my affectionate greeting."

I should have said nothing.

Instead I said, "If he wishes to hear it."

The Judge's expression brightened.

Hepzibah's did not.

I had not agreed to anything, yet I felt I had given something away.

The Judge spoke a little longer, all kindness and composure, then left.

When the bell stopped trembling behind him, Hepzibah began putting objects in order that were already in order.

"Why are you afraid of him?" I asked.

Her hands stopped.

"I am not afraid."

"Then why won't you let him see Clifford?"

"Because Clifford must not see him."

"That isn't the same thing."

"It is enough."

I was angry then.

Not at the Judge. At being expected to obey dangers no one would name.

"Everyone in this house keeps telling me what is enough."

Hepzibah turned toward me.

"Because there are some things you have not had to learn."

"Then tell me."

She looked suddenly old.

I regretted the demand, but not enough to withdraw it.

"Not now," she said.

Later I saw Holgrave in the garden and told him about the Judge.

He listened more seriously than I expected.

"He tried to kiss me."

Holgrave looked at me.

"And?"

"I stepped away."

"Good."

I bristled.

"I did not ask whether it was good."

"No."

"He was perfectly polite afterward."

"I imagine he was."

"You dislike him too."

Holgrave did not answer directly.

Instead he brought out a daguerreotype I had seen before.

The Judge's face.

Only it did not look like the face that had smiled at me in the shop.

The plate caught something harder.

"There," Holgrave said. "That is what the sun saw."

I looked at the image.

"The sun did not speak to him."

"No."

"It does not know what he has done."

"No."

"Then you cannot use it as a verdict."

Holgrave looked almost pleased.

"I didn't say verdict."

"You meant evidence."

"Perhaps."

I handed the plate back.

The picture unsettled me anyway.

That annoyed me.

That evening Clifford was in the parlor when I entered.

I nearly told him the Judge had called.

Then I remembered Hepzibah's face.

I said nothing.

For once, withholding knowledge felt less like exclusion and more like care.

But I still did not know what I was protecting him from.
