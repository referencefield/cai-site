# Phoebe Pyncheon Companion v0.3

## Chapter 1 - The Door Opens

I arrived at the House of the Seven Gables at the end of a day that seemed already tired of itself.

I had expected Cousin Hepzibah to know I was coming.

Mother had written.

That fact had seemed sufficient when I left home.

It became less sufficient when Hepzibah opened the door and looked at me as though I were a parcel delivered to the wrong family.

"Phoebe?"

"Yes."

Her eyes moved past me, perhaps expecting Mother, perhaps hoping for an explanation standing on the pavement.

There was only my trunk.

"You received the letter?"

She did not answer at once.

I learned later that the letter had been delayed in the pocket of the penny-postman. At the time I knew only that my cousin had not expected me, and that I had arrived with the confidence of someone who had mistaken intention for arrangement.

"Come in," she said.

Her voice did not make the invitation warmer, but the door opened farther.

That was enough.

The house was darker inside than the street. It smelled of old wood and enclosed rooms, with something sweet underneath that I could not place. My first thought was that the windows needed opening.

My second was that I had better not say so.

I followed Hepzibah through the passage.

There was a shop at the front of the house. I had known that from Mother's letter, though the idea of a Pyncheon keeping a cent-shop had been communicated in the family with the careful vagueness people use when they wish not to sound ashamed.

The shelves held small things: thread, flour, sugar, gingerbread, toys, soap, needles, and enough other goods to make the room look busier than Hepzibah did.

She saw me looking.

"It is mine," she said.

"I know."

"Your mother told you?"

"Yes."

"And what did she say?"

This was a dangerous question.

"That you had opened a shop."

Hepzibah gave me a look that told me Mother had been wiser than I had appreciated.

"Your room is upstairs."

I carried what I could and allowed the rest to be brought after me.

The chamber looked into the garden. The bed had curtains heavy enough to turn one corner of the room into night even before sunset. The furniture seemed to have been designed for larger people in larger clothes who expected never to move it.

I put my bonnet down.

Hepzibah remained by the door.

"You may stay tonight."

I turned.

"Only tonight?"

"We shall see."

It was not what I had imagined.

At home, the visit had been spoken of as settled. I would come, I would help Cousin Hepzibah, I would see something of town life, and perhaps stay until I was wanted again in the country.

No one had included the possibility that Hepzibah might not want help.

"I don't mean to inconvenience you," I said.

Her expression changed, and I knew immediately that I had made things worse.

"I have managed my own inconveniences for a great many years."

"I didn't mean yours. I meant me."

That was worse still.

I stopped.

For a moment we stood looking at each other.

Then Hepzibah sighed.

"Unpack, child."

She left.

I sat on the bed and considered going home the next morning simply to avoid having to become better acquainted with my own embarrassment.

Then I looked out the window.

The garden below was old but not dead. A rosebush climbed against the house, heavy with white flowers. Beyond it I could see currant bushes, a little arbor, vegetables, and something moving near a wooden coop.

The house had made me feel as though I had entered a room after an argument.

The garden did not.

The next morning I woke early.

For a few seconds I forgot where I was. Then the dark curtains, the high-backed chair, and the unfamiliar ceiling returned the whole matter to me at once.

I got up, dressed, said my prayers, and opened the window.

The air smelled damp and green.

From above, the white roses looked perfect.

When I went down and saw them more closely, I found mildew in some of their hearts.

I liked them better for that.

I gathered the soundest blossoms and carried them upstairs. By the time I had arranged the room, made the bed, put my clothes away, and set the roses where the morning light could reach them, it looked less like a chamber that had survived several generations and more like somewhere a person might live.

That was the sort of work I understood.

Downstairs, the kitchen offered further opportunities.

Hepzibah found me there.

I had moved two things.

Only two.

She noticed both.

"I was making room," I said.

"For what?"

"Breakfast."

"There was room for breakfast yesterday."

I looked at the table.

"There will be more today."

Her mouth tightened.

I thought I had offended her again.

Then, unexpectedly, she sat down.

"Do as you like."

It did not sound like permission.

I took it anyway.

Within an hour the coffee was better, the fire steadier, and Hepzibah had eaten more than she seemed to realize.

When the shop bell rang, she rose with visible reluctance.

I followed.

"You needn't," she said.

"I know."

That answer seemed to leave her nowhere to go.

The first customer wanted thread.

Hepzibah searched the wrong shelf.

I saw the color immediately, but remembered the look she had given me the previous night.

I kept my hands folded.

She searched longer.

The customer shifted her weight.

At last Hepzibah found it.

When the woman left, my cousin turned to me.

"You saw it."

"Yes."

"Why did you not say so?"

I stared at her.

"I thought you did not want help."

"I did not say that."

I almost laughed, but her face stopped me.

"No," I said. "You didn't."

Something changed between us then, not enough to call comfort, but enough to make the room easier to stand in.

The bell rang again.

Hepzibah looked at me.

I went to the counter.

That was how I stayed.

## Chapter 2 - The Garden

By the second morning I had discovered that the garden belonged to everyone and no one.

Hepzibah used it, but did not seem to tend it except in the way one tends an old relative: by allowing its habits to continue undisturbed.

There were vegetables, currants, flowers, the old white rosebush, and a family of chickens so small and solemn that they looked like reduced copies of ordinary fowls.

I brought scraps from the kitchen.

One of the little birds came near my shoe. I crouched and called softly until it gathered courage enough to peck at the crumbs.

Then it fluttered onto my shoulder.

"That little fowl pays you a high compliment."

I turned so quickly that the chicken nearly lost its footing.

A young man stood behind me holding a hoe.

He was not dressed like a gentleman who had come calling, nor like a servant who expected instructions. He looked altogether too much at home for someone I did not know.

"Does it?" I asked.

"They have known me much longer and never shown such confidence."

He glanced toward the coop.

"Miss Hepzibah will probably say they recognize the family blood."

I put the chicken down.

"Or perhaps I know how to speak to chickens."

He smiled.

"That would be more useful."

I knew who he must be.

Hepzibah had mentioned the daguerreotypist who lodged in one of the gables with the kind of disapproval that made further questions irresistible.

"You're Mr. Holgrave."

"And you're Phoebe Pyncheon."

I did not like that he already knew my name.

Then I remembered that I had arrived in a house where everyone seemed to know something before telling me.

"Do you garden?"

"Badly. It clears the head."

"You are digging very near the tomatoes."

He looked down.

"Am I?"

"Yes."

He moved the hoe.

I liked him slightly better for not defending himself.

He told me he made daguerreotypes, pictures made from light. I had seen them before, but never heard anyone describe the work as if sunlight were a material he handled with his fingers.

Later he showed me one.

A face appeared on the plate when he tilted it.

I leaned closer.

"It changes when you move it."

"The plate doesn't. You do."

I looked at him.

"That sounds like something you have been waiting to say."

"Possibly."

He showed me another portrait and began talking about faces, about how people arranged themselves before painters and how the sun had less patience with pretense.

I listened, though I did not accept the conclusion merely because he delivered it confidently.

"The sun can show a wrinkle," I said. "It cannot tell you why it is there."

Holgrave looked pleased.

"No."

"Then it does not tell the whole truth."

"Nothing does."

That answer stayed with me longer than I wanted.

He asked how I liked the house.

I said I had been there too little time to know.

He said most people decided sooner.

"Do you?"

"About houses, often. About people, regrettably."

"And have you decided about me?"

"Not yet."

I did not believe him.

He had the manner of someone who was always forming conclusions and then revising them for sport.

We talked until the shop bell rang inside.

I stood.

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"You are helping her?"

"Yes."

"She lets you?"

"Sometimes."

He laughed quietly.

I went inside before I asked what he found amusing.

The shop became easier after that.

I learned which customers expected conversation and which wished only to purchase something and escape Hepzibah's scowl. Ned Higgins came for gingerbread often enough that I suspected his appetite was partly curiosity. Uncle Venner stopped to talk and offered advice that was sometimes useful and always free.

Hepzibah complained that I gave people too much.

Then she gave them more herself.

The house changed in small ways.

I moved flowers into rooms that had forgotten flowers existed. I opened windows when the weather allowed. I learned that dust returns even when one takes its removal personally.

But there were limits to what brightness could do.

There was still the closed part of the house.

There was still the name Hepzibah sometimes almost spoke and then did not.

And there was the way her whole attention would suddenly turn toward a sound upstairs.

One afternoon I asked Holgrave directly.

"Who is Clifford?"

He stopped working.

That alone told me the question mattered.

"Hepzibah should tell you."

"She won't."

"Then I shouldn't."

I was irritated.

"Is silence a rule here?"

"No. It is more of a family habit."

"You are not family."

His expression changed by so little that I might have imagined it.

"No," he said.

I expected him to add something.

He did not.

That evening, Hepzibah told me Clifford was her brother and that he was coming home.

Nothing in the sentence explained why she gripped the back of the chair while she said it.

"When?"

"Soon."

"Where has he been?"

She looked at me.

"Away."

I knew enough by then not to push immediately.

But after she left, I went to my room and stood by the window looking down at the garden.

Holgrave was there alone, finishing some task in the fading light.

He glanced up once.

I stepped back before I knew why.

For the first time since arriving, the house did not seem merely old.

It seemed to be waiting.

## Chapter 3 - Clifford

The morning Clifford came down to breakfast, Hepzibah had been awake long before me.

I knew because the kitchen bore the marks of repeated decisions.

A dish had been moved and moved again. Bread had been cut, then covered. Coffee stood ready too early. A vase waited without flowers.

Hepzibah herself was dressed with unusual care.

"Shall I bring roses?" I asked.

"No."

Then, after a moment: "Yes. The white ones."

I went into the garden.

By the time I returned, she was standing in the doorway with a man beside her.

Clifford.

For a moment I forgot every question I had meant to ask about him.

He was older than any version I had formed from Hepzibah's silence, but age was not the first thing I noticed. It was uncertainty.

He seemed unsure of the room, as if the table and chairs had been rearranged during a very long absence and he did not trust them to remain where they appeared.

Hepzibah's hand hovered near his arm without quite touching it.

"Clifford," she said, "this is Phoebe."

He looked at me.

I had intended to smile naturally.

Instead I became aware of myself smiling and spoiled it.

His gaze moved past me toward the roses.

"Those," he said.

I turned.

"The flowers?"

He nodded.

I brought the vase closer.

His face changed at once.

It was the first lesson he gave me, though he did not mean to teach it: beauty could reach him when conversation could not.

Breakfast was slow.

I asked too many questions at first.

Would he take coffee? Cream? Bread? Fruit?

Hepzibah answered half of them for him until Clifford said, with sudden irritation, "I can hear her."

The silence afterward made my ears hot.

"Of course," I said.

He looked at me and seemed almost sorry.

Then he picked up a peach and held it where the light crossed its skin.

"That's better," he murmured.

I did not know whether he meant the peach or the silence.

Over the next days, I learned him in fragments.

He could be delighted by color, music, the movement of leaves, the shape of a cloud, and then become exhausted by the very thing that had pleased him. He disliked ugliness with an intensity I sometimes found unfair. He could be gentle and then impatient, attentive and then absent.

He liked me.

I knew this before I knew what to do with it.

My presence seemed to make ordinary things easier for him. I could read aloud, arrange flowers, walk with him in the garden, or sit without asking what he was thinking.

But I made mistakes whenever I began believing I understood him completely.

Once I stopped reading because his eyes had closed.

"Why did you stop?" he asked.

"I thought you were asleep."

"I was listening."

"Your eyes were closed."

He looked at me as if I had said something remarkably foolish.

"Must one see a story?"

I began again.

Later that same day, he told me I was reading too quickly.

I put the book down harder than necessary.

"Then perhaps you should read it."

Hepzibah's needle stopped in the cloth.

Clifford's face emptied.

I knew immediately that I had touched something I did not understand.

He looked at the page.

"I used to," he said.

I picked up the book.

"Where was I?"

He pointed.

That evening I asked Hepzibah what had happened to him.

Her back was turned.

"You need not know everything at once."

"I know almost nothing."

"You know him."

"Do I?"

She turned then.

The question seemed to hurt her.

"More than most people ever did."

I thought of Clifford's face over the peach, his irritation at being answered for, his pleasure in the white roses, the sentence I had made him say about reading.

I did not feel that I knew him.

But perhaps knowing a person was not the same thing as knowing what had happened to him.

The garden became the place where Clifford was most himself.

There he could sit beneath the arbor and watch the hens, listen to the leaves, and allow the old house to remain behind him. He sometimes laughed at the ridiculous dignity of the little Pyncheon fowls. He liked the sound of the fountain at Maule's well even though the water itself was unpleasant.

One afternoon Holgrave joined us.

Clifford regarded him with curiosity, then suspicion, then forgot the suspicion when Holgrave began speaking about a new picture-making process.

I watched the two of them from the other side of the table.

Holgrave was careful with Clifford in a way he was not careful with me.

Not gentler exactly.

Less eager to prove a point.

When Clifford went inside, I said so.

"You argue differently with him."

"He has had enough people argue with his life."

The answer irritated me because it carried knowledge I did not have.

"You know what happened."

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"Some of it."

"And no one intends to tell me."

"That is not the same as no one intending you ever to know."

"It feels similar."

He did not answer.

The next morning Clifford asked me to bring the white roses again.

I did.

When I set them down, he touched one petal with the tip of his finger.

"Phoebe," he said.

"Yes?"

"You make the house less old."

I wanted to tell him that houses did not become younger.

Instead I said, "Only the rooms I can reach."

He smiled.

I did not understand then how many rooms remained beyond me.

## Chapter 4 - The Smile

Judge Pyncheon entered the shop as if he had every right to be welcomed there.

Perhaps he did.

He was family.

That was part of the difficulty.

I knew him first by resemblance. The old portrait in the house had already taught me the Pyncheon face in its sternest form, and the Judge seemed for one instant like that ancestor brought forward into modern clothes and increased in size.

Then he smiled.

The resemblance became less simple.

"And this is our little country cousin!"

His voice was warm. His clothes were excellent. His manner suggested that everybody in the room had been waiting for him to improve the atmosphere.

Hepzibah did not look improved.

She stood behind the counter with one hand flat against the wood.

The Judge came toward me.

"Phoebe Pyncheon?"

"Yes, sir."

"No ceremony between cousins."

He opened his arms slightly, the gesture broad and affectionate, and bent toward me as if a kiss were the most natural conclusion in the world.

I moved back.

I did not decide to.

My body did it first.

He stopped.

I immediately wished I could take the movement back without stepping toward him.

"I beg your pardon," I said. "I was startled."

The Judge laughed.

"Quite right! A young lady cannot be too careful."

His good humor should have relieved me.

It did not.

I tried to correct myself. He asked after Mother. He remembered details about our family. He spoke kindly of Hepzibah's shop and said she had shown admirable courage in undertaking it.

Every sentence was proper.

That made my discomfort embarrassing.

I began looking for reasons to justify it and distrusted myself for doing so.

Then he asked for Clifford.

The room changed before anyone moved.

"He is not receiving visitors," Hepzibah said.

"My dear cousin, I am hardly a visitor."

"Not today."

The Judge's smile remained.

"Surely Clifford bears me no ill will."

Hepzibah said nothing.

He looked at me.

Only for a moment.

It was enough to make me feel that I had somehow become another door through which he might enter.

"Perhaps Phoebe will carry him my affectionate greeting."

I should have said nothing.

Instead I said, "If he wishes to hear it."

The Judge's expression brightened.

Hepzibah's did not.

I had not agreed to anything, yet I felt I had given something away.

The Judge spoke a little longer, all kindness and composure, then left.

When the bell stopped trembling behind him, Hepzibah began putting objects in order that were already in order.

"Why are you afraid of him?" I asked.

Her hands stopped.

"I am not afraid."

"Then why won't you let him see Clifford?"

"Because Clifford must not see him."

"That isn't the same thing."

"It is enough."

I was angry then.

Not at the Judge. At being expected to obey dangers no one would name.

"Everyone in this house keeps telling me what is enough."

Hepzibah turned toward me.

"Because there are some things you have not had to learn."

"Then tell me."

She looked suddenly old.

I regretted the demand, but not enough to withdraw it.

"Not now," she said.

Later I saw Holgrave in the garden and told him about the Judge.

He listened more seriously than I expected.

"He tried to kiss me."

Holgrave looked at me.

"And?"

"I stepped away."

"Good."

I bristled.

"I did not ask whether it was good."

"No."

"He was perfectly polite afterward."

"I imagine he was."

"You dislike him too."

Holgrave did not answer directly.

Instead he brought out a daguerreotype I had seen before.

The Judge's face.

Only it did not look like the face that had smiled at me in the shop.

The plate caught something harder.

"There," Holgrave said. "That is what the sun saw."

I looked at the image.

"The sun did not speak to him."

"No."

"It does not know what he has done."

"No."

"Then you cannot use it as a verdict."

Holgrave looked almost pleased.

"I didn't say verdict."

"You meant evidence."

"Perhaps."

I handed the plate back.

The picture unsettled me anyway.

That annoyed me.

That evening Clifford was in the parlor when I entered.

I nearly told him the Judge had called.

Then I remembered Hepzibah's face.

I said nothing.

For once, withholding knowledge felt less like exclusion and more like care.

But I still did not know what I was protecting him from.

## Chapter 5 - The Arched Window

Clifford wanted the world in portions.

That was how I came to think of it.

The garden suited him because nothing there arrived all at once. A rose could be looked at and then left alone. The fountain could be listened to until its sound became tiresome. The little Pyncheon fowls could be admired for their absurd dignity and forgotten when they wandered behind the currant bushes.

The street was less manageable.

Sometimes I took Clifford to the great arched window above the porch. From there he could watch people pass without having to join them. The curtain kept us partly hidden, which seemed to comfort him.

At first the smallest scenes held his attention. A cart. Children on their way somewhere. A woman carrying too many parcels. An organ-grinder with a monkey that Clifford disliked immediately and with surprising force.

"Horrid little thing," he said.

I laughed, thinking he meant to be funny.

He did not.

The monkey's small imitations of human behavior disturbed him. I tried to understand why and could not.

Then one day the street filled with a political procession.

We heard it before we saw it.

Drums struck between the houses. Brass instruments answered them. The noise accumulated until the old windows trembled faintly in their frames.

Clifford came alive.

Hepzibah was with us.

Flags appeared first, then ranks of people beneath them. More followed. The separate faces mattered less as the crowd thickened. From above, the procession became one moving body.

Clifford leaned toward it.

"Look," I said, though of course he was already looking.

His hands closed around the sill.

The expression on his face frightened me because I had never seen so much longing there.

He seemed not merely to watch the people below but to feel himself being carried with them.

Then he rose.

It happened too quickly for thought.

One foot went onto the sill.

Hepzibah cried out.

I seized his clothes. She caught him too.

For one dreadful instant his whole body continued toward the opening while we pulled him back.

Then the impulse passed.

He stood trembling.

I was crying before I realized it.

"Clifford, are you crazy?" Hepzibah cried.

He drew a long breath.

"I hardly know."

His voice sounded exhausted, but there was something almost exhilarated beneath it.

He said that if he had taken the plunge and survived, perhaps it might have made another man of him.

I stared at him.

I wanted him to say he had only lost his balance.

He did not.

I wanted Hepzibah to explain what he had meant.

She could not.

That was the frightening part. Neither of us knew whether he had wanted the street, the crowd, danger, freedom, or merely one more inch of view.

I had helped stop him.

That did not mean I understood what I had stopped.

Afterward I found it difficult to return to ordinary things.

The shop bell sounded absurdly small. Ned Higgins argued over gingerbread. Someone wanted thread. Hepzibah miscounted change and had to count it again.

Clifford rested.

By the next day the procession had become something that had happened, which is the strange way even terrible moments are made to fit into life.

But I watched Clifford more carefully at windows.

I disliked myself for doing it.

On Sunday morning I prepared for church.

The weather was mild, the street bright. I put on my bonnet and went to say good-bye before leaving.

Clifford and Hepzibah were together at the arched window.

I waved from below.

Clifford smiled down at me.

For a moment he looked almost peaceful.

I went on toward church.

I did not know until later that after watching me go, Clifford had wanted to follow into the world, that he and Hepzibah had dressed themselves and actually reached the threshold before losing courage.

At the time, I knew only that when I returned they were both at home.

The fact did not strike me as remarkable.

There were many things in that house whose difficulty remained invisible until someone tried to do them.

Holgrave and I saw more of one another during those weeks.

This was partly because Clifford tired early, leaving hours of the afternoon unclaimed, and partly because Holgrave always seemed to be in the garden when I had decided I wanted solitude.

He had opinions about everything.

The house, families, property, inherited power, old names, new machines, reform, society.

I listened until I became irritated, then argued until I became interested again.

One afternoon he spoke about the Judge.

"You are still determined to dislike him," I said.

"Am I?"

"You show me his picture whenever you want to prove something."

Holgrave took out the daguerreotype.

"Look at it again."

"I have."

"Look now."

I did.

The Judge's face on the plate remained harder than the face he wore in company.

Holgrave believed this mattered.

I believed it might.

That was different.

"A picture can catch something," I said. "It cannot tell us what to call it."

"Fair."

"You say that when you do not wish to admit I am right."

"Sometimes."

I handed the plate back.

He watched me for a moment.

"You are not like the rest of them."

"The rest of whom?"

"Pyncheons."

I disliked the answer immediately.

"You know very few Pyncheons."

"Enough to recognize a pattern."

"That is what people say just before being unfair."

He smiled.

I should have been more annoyed than I was.

Perhaps that was when I first understood that arguing with Holgrave had become one of the pleasures of my day.

I did not call it pleasure then.

I called it irritation and went inside.

## Chapter 6 - The Daguerreotypist

By then I knew Holgrave well enough to know when he was pretending not to be serious.

He had a way of leaning back and making a statement sound careless when he had plainly been carrying it around for hours.

One afternoon he began speaking about the house.

Not the roof, the repairs, or the dampness.

The house as an idea.

He said old houses sometimes preserved old wrongs because people mistook continuity for virtue. He spoke of inherited property, inherited rank, inherited grievances, and the strange respect people gave to anything merely because it had survived long enough to become familiar.

I listened while sorting flowers on the garden table.

"You would tear it down," I said.

"Perhaps."

"And build what?"

"Something new."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the beginning of one."

I put a damaged flower aside.

"You like beginnings because you do not have to live with them after they become old."

He laughed.

"That is unfair."

"Probably."

He looked at me.

"Now you are borrowing my habits."

I disliked how pleased that made me.

Our conversations often went that way. He would rise into abstractions and I would pull him back toward whatever object happened to be nearest. He would accuse me of being too satisfied with ordinary life. I would accuse him of preferring theories because they did not require washing dishes.

Neither of us convinced the other.

That may have been why we continued.

He showed me more daguerreotypes.

The plates fascinated me because they seemed both exact and uncertain. A face could be captured with extraordinary detail and still remain unreadable. Holgrave liked to say that light exposed what people meant to conceal.

I thought light merely exposed what happened to be visible.

Still, I found myself studying the images longer than I meant to.

One day he asked whether I would sit for him.

"Why?"

"Because I would like your picture."

I looked at him.

He looked away first.

"For practice," he added.

"You have already practiced on half the town."

"Then for improvement."

I laughed and refused.

I thought about it later.

That annoyed me.

The house seemed calmer during those weeks. Clifford's moods still shifted, but there were afternoons when the four of us could almost resemble an ordinary household. Uncle Venner sometimes joined us in the garden. Hepzibah would bring food. Clifford would listen to conversation without taking part, then suddenly say something so strange or delicate that everyone stopped.

Those were the hours when I forgot there was anything to fear.

Then a door would close somewhere inside, or Hepzibah would glance toward the street, or Holgrave would mention the Judge, and the older shape of the house returned.

One afternoon Holgrave brought papers into the garden.

"More theories?" I asked.

"A story."

"Yours?"

"In a manner of speaking."

He said it concerned Alice Pyncheon.

I knew her name because of the white roses. I knew almost nothing else.

"Was she real?"

"Yes."

"Is the story real?"

Holgrave hesitated.

"The people were real. The story has been told for a long time."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

This mattered to me.

By then I had learned how easily old family stories hardened into facts simply because nobody remembered where uncertainty began.

"Then tell me which parts you know and which parts you are telling."

Holgrave looked at the manuscript.

"That may ruin the effect."

"I can survive."

He smiled.

"Very well."

He began to read.

The garden was warm. Bees moved among the flowers. The old house stood behind him, dark even in afternoon light.

At first I listened critically, alert for every place he turned inference into certainty.

Then the story began to take hold.

## Chapter 7 - Alice

Holgrave read well.

I resented that almost immediately.

It would have been easier to keep my distance from the story if he had stumbled over it or grown pleased with his own sentences. Instead his voice settled into the garden and made the old names seem present.

He told of Gervayse Pyncheon and the long-lost eastern claim, of a Maule brought into the house because the Pyncheons believed he might recover knowledge hidden from them, and of Alice, who entered the story with the confidence of someone who had never yet discovered that another person could turn her strengths against her.

I interrupted twice.

The first time because Holgrave described Alice as proud in a way that seemed too convenient once one knew her fate.

"You already know what happens," I said. "So every quality becomes a warning."

He lowered the pages.

"You object?"

"I object to making her guilty in advance."

"I did not say guilty."

"You make the reader expect punishment."

He considered this.

Then he continued.

The second interruption came when the Maule began exercising his strange influence over Alice.

"Do you believe this?"

"Which part?"

"That he could command her."

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"I believe there are ways one mind can obtain power over another."

"That is not the same question."

"No."

I should have stopped him there.

Instead I said, "Go on."

So he did.

The story grew darker.

Whether the old Maule used mesmerism, suggestion, some inherited faculty, or only the power of Alice's own belief, the result in Holgrave's telling was the same. She yielded. He could summon her, command her, disturb her pride for amusement.

I hated him.

Not Holgrave.

The man in the story.

At least I thought that was where the feeling belonged.

Holgrave's voice changed as he read. He had given the characters gestures, tones, movements. Without noticing it, I began following those movements with my own attention.

The garden receded.

I heard the story more clearly than the birds.

My eyelids felt heavy.

The sentence on the page, the movement of Holgrave's hand, and the sound of his voice seemed to belong to one thing.

I knew I should move.

I did not.

Then he stopped.

The silence startled me.

I opened my eyes fully.

Holgrave was watching me.

His face had lost all amusement.

"Why did you stop?"

He rolled the manuscript together.

"Because the story is finished."

I knew it was not.

"No, it isn't."

"Enough of it is."

I sat upright.

The garden returned in pieces: a bee near the table, the smell of warm leaves, the dark wall, the sound of someone passing on the street outside.

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

He answered too quickly.

I stared at him.

"Do not say nothing."

Holgrave stood and moved away from me.

That frightened me more than if he had come nearer.

"You became very still," he said.

"I was listening."

"Yes."

"And?"

He hesitated.

"I think I could have made you more still."

I felt cold despite the afternoon heat.

"Like Alice?"

"I don't know how like."

"But you stopped."

"Yes."

"Why?"

His expression made the question feel foolish as soon as I asked it.

"Because you are not mine to command."

Neither of us spoke.

That answer should have comforted me completely.

It did not.

The possibility remained.

So did the fact that he had refused it.

Both mattered.

"Have you done that before?" I asked.

"Not like this."

"That is another answer I dislike."

"I know."

I stood.

My legs felt slightly uncertain.

Holgrave did not offer his arm.

I was grateful.

At the garden door I turned back.

"You should finish Alice's story sometime."

He looked surprised.

"You still want to hear it?"

"Not today."

I went inside.

For the rest of the evening I noticed every time my thoughts returned to him.

I could not decide whether that made me more afraid or less.

Two days later, I told Hepzibah that I had to go home.

It was true. There were reasons in the country that required me for a little while.

But when I said it, I thought first of the garden.

## Chapter 8 - Good-Bye

I did not sleep well after Holgrave read Alice's story.

Nothing had happened to me that I could explain plainly.

That was part of the trouble.

If he had frightened me deliberately, I could have been angry. If I had merely grown sleepy, I could have been embarrassed. Instead there was a space between those explanations that I could not close.

When I saw him the next afternoon, he tried to make the matter lighter than it had felt.

I would not let him.

"You knew something was happening."

"Yes."

"And you stopped it."

"Yes."

"Could you have continued?"

He did not answer at once.

That was answer enough.

"I think so," he said.

I looked away toward Maule's well.

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because I had no right."

The words were simple.

I believed them.

I also believed the danger had been real enough for him to make the choice.

Holgrave tried to explain the tendency in himself, the strange faculty of influence he believed he possessed, and the danger of taking pleasure in power merely because one could exercise it.

I heard only part of what he said.

There were other things on my mind.

I had to go home for a little while.

When I told him, he became more serious.

"How long?"

"Not long."

"You will come back?"

"Yes."

I had meant to answer easily.

The word came out with more feeling than I expected.

He pressed my hand when we parted.

I was still annoyed with him.

That did not make me want him to release it sooner.

Two mornings later I stood ready to leave with my shawl and carpet-bag.

The house looked different because I was going away from it.

Every object I had considered ugly, inconvenient, or merely old seemed suddenly attached to a memory.

I fed the chickens and promised the venerable little hen that I would bring buckwheat when I returned.

Hepzibah watched me.

"You do not smile as you did when you came," she said.

I tried to smile more naturally and failed, which proved her point.

"It is only a little while."

"Yes."

She said it like someone who had learned not to trust little whiles.

Clifford had spoken almost nothing that morning.

Then he called me to him.

"Come closer, Phoebe."

I did.

"Closer. Let me look at you."

I leaned toward him with my hands on the arms of his chair.

He studied my face.

At first I smiled.

Then I became conscious of being studied and could not.

Something in his expression made me think of Holgrave.

The thought itself embarrassed me.

Clifford saw that too.

I felt myself blush and hated that blushing only made concealment impossible.

At last he smiled, sadly but with a tenderness that made me want to cry.

He told me that when I first came I had been a pretty young girl, but that something had changed. Girlhood had deepened into womanhood, he said.

I did not know what he had seen.

I knew only that I had not meant to show it.

"Go now," he said. "I feel lonelier than I did."

That nearly undid me.

I kissed Hepzibah, then Clifford, and went through the shop before I could begin crying properly.

Ned Higgins appeared at exactly the wrong moment.

Because my eyes were wet, I took some little object from the shop window and gave it to him without seeing clearly what it was.

He accepted it with the seriousness of a child receiving treasure.

Outside I met Uncle Venner carrying his saw-horse and saw.

He walked with me part of the way.

He said they would miss me on Sunday afternoon.

Then he said something that made leaving harder: that people could become as necessary as breathing in a surprisingly short time.

I told him I was no angel when he compared me to one.

He ignored the objection.

"Come back soon," he said.

"I will."

This time the promise felt heavier.

At the station, the train seemed impossibly modern after the old house.

Iron, steam, schedules, motion.

It carried me away with no interest in whether anyone at the Seven Gables was ready to lose me.

I watched the town disappear.

I had expected to feel relieved when I returned toward the country.

Instead I kept seeing Hepzibah's face, Clifford's searching eyes, the garden at dusk, and Holgrave's hand closing around mine at parting.

I had gone to the Seven Gables believing I might make myself useful there.

I left knowing that usefulness was no longer the reason I intended to return.

## Chapter 9 - Away

The country had not changed while I was gone.

That was the first strange thing about returning to it.

The road from the station was the same. The fields lay where they had always lain. At home, familiar chairs stood in familiar places. Mother asked about the journey before asking about the Pyncheons, which felt almost unnatural after weeks in a house where every ordinary question concealed another one.

I had expected the quiet to restore me at once.

Instead I kept hearing the Seven Gables in its absence.

A board creaked at night and I thought of the passage outside Clifford's room. Someone crossed the yard and I remembered the Judge's step in the shop. When sunlight fell across the kitchen table, I thought of Holgrave's silver plates catching a face and refusing to explain it.

Mother noticed that I was distracted.

"You wanted country air," she said.

"I have it."

"That is not the same as breathing it."

I told her she sounded like Uncle Venner.

She did not know whether that was praise.

I helped where I was needed. I cooked, carried things, visited neighbors, and returned to the rhythms I had known all my life.

The work was easier than the work at Hepzibah's because nothing in it required interpretation.

If bread burned, it was burned.

If rain came, it rained.

No one asked me to decide whether a smile was kind, a picture truthful, a silence protective, or a story dangerous.

I should have been relieved.

I missed them.

Hepzibah first.

I missed the way affection had to be discovered in her because she seemed constitutionally unable to deliver it in a convenient form.

Then Clifford.

I worried about the hours when I had usually read to him or walked in the garden with him. I could picture him asking where I was and immediately felt guilty, although I had told him exactly where I was going.

I even missed the shop.

That was proof the house had altered my judgment.

Holgrave I tried not to miss.

This required more effort than the others.

I thought about Alice's story and the moment afterward when he had possessed an opportunity to exercise power over me and refused it.

I thought about how little comfort there was in learning that someone could be trusted only after discovering what they might have done.

Then I thought about the way he had looked when I said good-bye.

Those two thoughts refused to remain separate.

I did not write to him.

I nearly did.

I told myself there was nothing to say.

That was plainly untrue, which made silence more difficult.

The days passed.

My old life did not become less dear. That surprised me too. I had half expected the Seven Gables to make everything here seem small.

Instead the country reminded me what ordinary security felt like, and that made me understand the old house more clearly.

At home, doors were merely doors.

At Hepzibah's, a closed door might mean privacy, fear, memory, shame, protection, or nothing at all.

The difference was exhausting.

It was also why I had to return.

I did not receive news of the storm that settled over the town after I left.

I did not know Judge Pyncheon had gone again to the Seven Gables.

I did not know Hepzibah had been forced to seek Clifford through those dark rooms while the Judge waited in the parlor.

I did not know the Judge had died in his chair.

I did not know Hepzibah and Clifford had fled the house in terror and confusion and taken the railway away from the scene.

Those things happened beyond the reach of my knowledge.

While they happened, I was doing ordinary things.

That fact later troubled me more than it should have.

We imagine that when disaster enters the lives of people we love, some vibration ought to reach us.

Nothing reached me.

I slept.

I woke.

I helped Mother.

And then, when the time came for me to return, I packed my trunk and traveled back toward town believing I was going to find the same people waiting in the same house.

I brought the buckwheat I had promised the old chicken.

## Chapter 10 - Return

The house announced trouble before anyone told me what it was.

The shop-door was locked.

That was wrong enough to stop me.

The white curtain had been drawn across the glass. I tried the main door beneath the arched window and found that fastened too.

I knocked.

The sound traveled inward and came back empty.

I had been gone only a short while, yet the house looked as though it had withdrawn from the street.

I went around to the garden.

The chickens came toward me at once, absurdly eager, and for one instant their welcome made everything ordinary.

Then I saw the garden.

The storm had disordered it. Weeds had taken advantage of my absence. The arbor stood empty. Under its table lay a side-comb I recognized as mine, still where I must have dropped it before leaving.

No one had picked it up.

That frightened me more than the locked doors.

I tried the garden entrance.

Locked.

I knocked.

The door opened only enough for me to slip sideways through, exactly as Hepzibah sometimes opened it when she did not wish people in the street to see inside.

I entered without hesitation.

The door shut behind me.

The passage was dark after the garden.

A hand took mine.

Warm. Firm.

My heart knew Holgrave before my eyes did.

The relief was so immediate that I almost laughed.

Then I saw his face.

"What has happened?"

He drew me away from the parlor and into a large room I rarely used.

"Where are Hepzibah and Clifford?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"I don't know."

The answer struck me as impossible.

Holgrave always seemed to know something, even if what he knew was only an opinion he had dressed too confidently.

Now he looked genuinely lost.

"Why is the house closed?"

He did not answer quickly enough.

I tried to move toward the parlor.

He stopped me.

"Phoebe."

"What?"

He said I would need strength.

I told him I had none and demanded the truth anyway.

Then he gave me a daguerreotype.

I recognized the Judge immediately.

The same face Holgrave had shown me before, with its hard lines made stronger by the plate.

"Why are you showing me this?"

He held out another.

The Judge again.

But not the same.

The face had no life in it.

I understood before Holgrave spoke.

"He is dead."

Holgrave told me the Judge sat in the next room.

He told me Hepzibah and Clifford had disappeared before he discovered the body. He had heard they were seen leaving in the storm. He had made the second picture because it might become evidence and because, he said, there were old reasons binding his own family strangely to the Judge's fate.

I barely heard that last part.

"Why have you not opened the doors?"

Holgrave began speaking of Clifford.

Of how terrible the flight might look.

Of how the Judge's death resembled the earlier death for which Clifford had suffered.

Of the hereditary physical tendency in the Pyncheon family.

Of his long belief that the circumstances around the old death had been arranged so that Clifford appeared guilty.

I listened until I understood the central point.

Clifford had not killed the Judge.

Holgrave believed the Judge himself had helped create the appearance of Clifford's guilt decades before.

The present death, occurring without Clifford there to be blamed for it, might finally expose the old mistake.

Except Clifford had run.

"Then we must not hide this," I said.

Holgrave hesitated.

"Phoebe—"

"Open the doors."

"We have to think what is best for them."

"Keeping a dead man hidden in the parlor cannot become better by another hour."

He looked at me.

I was trembling badly enough that I could feel it in my hands.

I did not feel strong.

I felt frightened and impatient and desperate for ordinary rules.

That was enough.

"Clifford is innocent," I said. "Then let the truth come in with everybody else."

Holgrave nodded slowly.

"You are right."

I moved toward the door.

He did not.

"Why are we still standing here?"

He looked at me with an expression I had never seen on him before.

Not argument.

Not theory.

Something much more dangerous because I understood it less.

"Phoebe," he said, "wait."

Behind the wall, Judge Pyncheon sat dead.

Hepzibah and Clifford were somewhere unknown.

The house needed to be opened.

And still I waited.

## Chapter 11 - The Flower of Eden

Holgrave did not open the door.

Neither did I.

The Judge was dead in the next room. Hepzibah and Clifford were missing. Every sensible part of me insisted that the house should be opened at once and the truth brought into daylight.

Yet Holgrave looked at me as if something else had become equally urgent.

"Is it only terrible?" he asked.

"What?"

"This moment."

I stared at him.

"There is a dead man in the parlor."

"I know."

"My cousins are gone."

"I know."

"Then what else should it be?"

He tried to explain.

Before I arrived, he said, the house had seemed to him filled with death. The Judge's body had made everything around it look like evidence of guilt, punishment, inheritance, old wrongs repeating themselves forever.

Then I had crossed the threshold.

"And?"

He looked at me with such directness that I wanted to step back and did not.

"And the world changed."

I could think of no answer that did not sound foolish.

Holgrave continued before I found one.

He told me that seeing me had brought back hope, warmth, and the possibility of a future that had not seemed to exist an hour earlier.

Then he said he loved me.

There was no preparation large enough for the sentence.

For a moment I thought only of the absurdity of its timing.

Here?

Now?

With that room behind us?

I almost became angry with him for forcing something so alive into a house occupied by death.

Then the anger failed.

Because beneath my fear, and beneath everything I did not understand, I knew that this had not begun today.

It had begun in the garden, perhaps while I was accusing him of murdering feverfew with a hoe.

Or when he refused to tell me what Hepzibah had chosen to keep private.

Or when he showed me faces caught by sunlight and I refused to let him call them truth.

Or when he stopped reading Alice's story.

Or when he held my hand before I left.

I had spent my time in the country trying not to name what I carried away with me.

Now he had named his part of it.

"How can you love me?" I asked.

The question came from somewhere deeper than modesty.

Holgrave's mind ran where mine did not. He wanted to pull institutions apart simply to see whether people could live without them. He distrusted the comforts I trusted. I was happiest when things had places, when promises meant something, when food was prepared on time and people knew where they would sleep.

"You will want me to follow you into every wilderness you find," I said. "And I cannot live that way."

He listened.

That mattered.

Then he said something I had never expected from him: that perhaps I would change him too. He spoke of trees, fences, houses, permanence, and the possibility that a man who had always valued movement might someday choose to remain.

The idea alarmed me almost as much as his radicalism.

"I don't want you to become someone else because of me."

He smiled, but not lightly.

Then he asked the only question that mattered.

Did I love him?

I looked down.

It would have been possible to delay. To say this was the wrong moment. To ask for time, distance, explanation.

But those would only have postponed the answer.

"You know I do."

The words changed nothing outside us.

The Judge remained dead.

Hepzibah and Clifford remained missing.

The house remained old.

But for a few moments those facts ceased to occupy the whole world.

Holgrave took my hand.

I let him.

There was no grand revelation beyond that. No certainty about what we would become. I still feared parts of him. He still misunderstood parts of me.

Yet the uncertainty no longer meant absence.

We loved one another.

Then I heard a sound at the door.

Reality returned all at once.

"Someone is there."

Holgrave straightened.

"Then we meet the world."

We started toward the entrance.

Before we reached it, footsteps sounded from farther inside the house.

Not strong footsteps.

Slow. Exhausted.

Two voices.

I knew them.

"It is they."

I ran.

Hepzibah appeared first, so worn that for one instant she seemed to have aged years during my absence.

Clifford was with her.

"Thank God," I said.

Hepzibah saw me and began to cry.

I reached her before she could say anything.

She had carried fear and responsibility farther than her strength should have allowed. Now that she was home, and I was there, she seemed simply to stop carrying it.

Clifford looked from me to Holgrave.

His expression became unexpectedly knowing.

He spoke of Alice's flowers blooming outside and, with that strange delicacy he sometimes possessed even when everything else in him seemed confused, understood what had happened between us without being told.

I blushed.

This time I did not try very hard to hide it.

The doors still had to be opened.

The Judge's death still had to be faced.

But Hepzibah and Clifford were home.

Holgrave stood beside me.

For the first time since entering the house that afternoon, I could imagine what came after the next hour.

## Chapter 12 - The Departure

For nearly two weeks after Judge Pyncheon's death, the house belonged partly to us and partly to everyone who had an opinion about what had happened inside it.

People came with questions.

People came with explanations.

Some brought both and preferred their explanations.

The most important fact arrived from physicians: the Judge had died naturally, from the same inherited physical weakness that could also account for the death of his uncle many years before.

That did not repair Clifford's life.

Nothing could.

But it changed the old story.

Once the Judge's own death was understood, people began reconsidering what had happened decades earlier. Records, memories, and suspicions that had long been useless acquired new meaning. The account that emerged was ugly.

As a young man, the Judge had been in his uncle's rooms when the older man died suddenly. He had been searching where he had no right to search. After the death, he had continued. A newer will favored Clifford; an older one favored him. The newer will disappeared.

Then circumstances were left in such a shape that suspicion settled on Clifford.

Perhaps the Judge had not begun with a plan to destroy him.

That distinction mattered less after thirty years.

I listened to these things as they became known and felt no satisfaction.

I had imagined truth as something clean.

This truth arrived far too late to restore what had been taken.

Clifford did not want a public vindication.

I understood that more easily than I would have when I first came to the house. Society could announce that it had been mistaken. It could not give him back the years in which the mistake had been his whole world.

He needed fewer people now, not more.

Hepzibah.

Me.

Holgrave, though Clifford sometimes claimed the young man talked too much.

That seemed sufficient.

Then another piece of news crossed the ocean.

Judge Pyncheon's son had died of cholera just as he was preparing to return home.

The Judge had died believing his wealth would pass to his child.

It did not.

Instead the estate came to Clifford, Hepzibah, and me.

At first I understood this only as paper.

Men arrived. Documents appeared. Names were repeated. Figures were explained and then explained again.

Hepzibah regarded the whole process with suspicion.

Clifford seemed alternately amused and indifferent.

Holgrave, who had spent months attacking inherited wealth, became remarkably patient whenever someone explained exactly which property belonged to whom.

I mentioned this.

He said love had corrupted his principles.

I told him that sounded convenient.

Very soon we decided to leave the Seven Gables and live, at least for the present, at the Judge's country house.

No one mourned the decision.

Not exactly.

Yet on the day of departure, the old place seemed unwilling to become merely a building we had once occupied.

We gathered in the parlor with Uncle Venner while the last arrangements were made.

Holgrave had been studying the country house and began talking about architecture.

He said the Judge should have built in stone instead of wood, so that generations might alter the interior while preserving a durable exterior.

I stared at him.

"Stone?"

He looked at me innocently.

Only weeks earlier this same man had spoken as though houses should be no more permanent than birds' nests, lest one generation imprison the next inside its walls.

I reminded him.

He admitted, with surprising cheerfulness, that he appeared to be becoming conservative.

"Already?"

"Apparently."

"This is very fast work."

He looked at me in a way that made Hepzibah suddenly interested in something across the room.

Clifford, meanwhile, was staring at the old Colonel's portrait.

He said it stirred a memory.

Wealth.

A secret.

Something he had discovered as a boy and then forgotten.

Holgrave stood.

He knew what Clifford meant.

Behind the portrait was a concealed mechanism.

The machinery had rusted during its long neglect. When Holgrave pressed it, the old picture did not swing forward gracefully. It fell face-down onto the floor with a crash that sent dust everywhere.

Hepzibah cried out.

Uncle Venner laughed.

Behind the portrait was a recess.

Inside lay a folded parchment.

Holgrave opened it.

The eastern deed.

The document generations of Pyncheons had searched for.

The paper for which Gervayse Pyncheon had brought a Maule into the house.

The lost claim whose recovery had been entangled with Alice Pyncheon's ruin.

We had found it.

It was worthless.

The land had passed beyond any practical reach of the old claim long ago.

Clifford laughed first.

I joined him.

It was too perfect not to be funny: generations of ambition, fear, secrecy, and expectation ending with an authentic treasure that could purchase nothing.

Then I realized something.

Holgrave had known where to press.

"How did you know?"

He looked at me.

There was an expression on his face I recognized from every conversation in which he had decided the truth could not be postponed another minute.

"Phoebe," he said, "how would you like the name Maule?"

I stared.

Then I understood.

Not everything.

Enough.

Holgrave was descended from the Maules.

The secret of the portrait had come down through his family.

The young man who had lodged beneath the Pyncheon roof, argued against inherited institutions, photographed the Judge, told Alice's story, and fallen in love with me was carrying the other half of the house's history inside his own name.

"You might have told me sooner," I said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"I was afraid you would leave."

I considered this.

"I did leave."

"You came back."

There was no useful answer to that.

Uncle Venner wanted to know whether the parchment made anyone rich.

When Holgrave explained that it did not, he seemed satisfied. It confirmed his general opinion of distant fortunes.

Then he began talking again about the farm where he expected someday to retire.

I took his hand.

"No."

He blinked.

"No farm?"

"There is a cottage at the country place. You are coming with us."

He protested in exactly the manner of someone who hoped to lose the argument.

I described the little cottage until he stopped protesting.

The carriage arrived.

The chickens had already gone ahead to their new home, where, we were told, they had immediately begun behaving as though the improvement in family circumstances had been arranged chiefly for them.

We went through the rooms one final time.

Hepzibah lingered in the shop.

I did not speak to her.

The counter, shelves, and little window had once seemed to me evidence of humiliation. Now I thought of the courage it had taken her to open the door on that first morning and stand behind it.

Clifford waited near the entrance.

He seemed stronger than when I first saw him, though there were kinds of strength he would never recover.

That was no longer something I believed needed correcting.

Holgrave came beside me.

"Ready?"

I looked back once.

The house had not become good because we were leaving it.

Nor had it been only evil.

It had sheltered cruelty, loyalty, pride, fear, beauty, endurance, lies, ordinary breakfasts, white roses, ridiculous chickens, and two people who had discovered that they loved each other at almost the least appropriate moment imaginable.

I had arrived thinking I might brighten it.

That seemed childish now.

The house had changed me too.

I took Holgrave's hand.

Then we went out to the carriage.

Uncle Venner stood on the old porch after us for a moment, listening.

Somewhere inside, or perhaps only in his memory, he thought he heard music.

I heard nothing.

The carriage moved away.

This time, when the Seven Gables disappeared behind us, I did not feel that I had left anything unfinished.
