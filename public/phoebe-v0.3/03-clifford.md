# Chapter 3 - Clifford

The morning Clifford came down to breakfast, Hepzibah had been awake long before me.

I knew because the kitchen bore the marks of repeated decisions.

A dish had been moved and moved again. Bread had been cut, then covered. Coffee stood ready too early. A vase waited without flowers.

Hepzibah herself was dressed with unusual care.

"Shall I bring roses?" I asked.

"No."

Then, after a moment: "Yes. The white ones."

I went into the garden.

By the time I returned, she was standing in the doorway with a man beside her.

Clifford.

For a moment I forgot every question I had meant to ask about him.

He was older than any version I had formed from Hepzibah's silence, but age was not the first thing I noticed. It was uncertainty.

He seemed unsure of the room, as if the table and chairs had been rearranged during a very long absence and he did not trust them to remain where they appeared.

Hepzibah's hand hovered near his arm without quite touching it.

"Clifford," she said, "this is Phoebe."

He looked at me.

I had intended to smile naturally.

Instead I became aware of myself smiling and spoiled it.

His gaze moved past me toward the roses.

"Those," he said.

I turned.

"The flowers?"

He nodded.

I brought the vase closer.

His face changed at once.

It was the first lesson he gave me, though he did not mean to teach it: beauty could reach him when conversation could not.

Breakfast was slow.

I asked too many questions at first.

Would he take coffee? Cream? Bread? Fruit?

Hepzibah answered half of them for him until Clifford said, with sudden irritation, "I can hear her."

The silence afterward made my ears hot.

"Of course," I said.

He looked at me and seemed almost sorry.

Then he picked up a peach and held it where the light crossed its skin.

"That's better," he murmured.

I did not know whether he meant the peach or the silence.

Over the next days, I learned him in fragments.

He could be delighted by color, music, the movement of leaves, the shape of a cloud, and then become exhausted by the very thing that had pleased him. He disliked ugliness with an intensity I sometimes found unfair. He could be gentle and then impatient, attentive and then absent.

He liked me.

I knew this before I knew what to do with it.

My presence seemed to make ordinary things easier for him. I could read aloud, arrange flowers, walk with him in the garden, or sit without asking what he was thinking.

But I made mistakes whenever I began believing I understood him completely.

Once I stopped reading because his eyes had closed.

"Why did you stop?" he asked.

"I thought you were asleep."

"I was listening."

"Your eyes were closed."

He looked at me as if I had said something remarkably foolish.

"Must one see a story?"

I began again.

Later that same day, he told me I was reading too quickly.

I put the book down harder than necessary.

"Then perhaps you should read it."

Hepzibah's needle stopped in the cloth.

Clifford's face emptied.

I knew immediately that I had touched something I did not understand.

He looked at the page.

"I used to," he said.

I picked up the book.

"Where was I?"

He pointed.

That evening I asked Hepzibah what had happened to him.

Her back was turned.

"You need not know everything at once."

"I know almost nothing."

"You know him."

"Do I?"

She turned then.

The question seemed to hurt her.

"More than most people ever did."

I thought of Clifford's face over the peach, his irritation at being answered for, his pleasure in the white roses, the sentence I had made him say about reading.

I did not feel that I knew him.

But perhaps knowing a person was not the same thing as knowing what had happened to him.

The garden became the place where Clifford was most himself.

There he could sit beneath the arbor and watch the hens, listen to the leaves, and allow the old house to remain behind him. He sometimes laughed at the ridiculous dignity of the little Pyncheon fowls. He liked the sound of the fountain at Maule's well even though the water itself was unpleasant.

One afternoon Holgrave joined us.

Clifford regarded him with curiosity, then suspicion, then forgot the suspicion when Holgrave began speaking about a new picture-making process.

I watched the two of them from the other side of the table.

Holgrave was careful with Clifford in a way he was not careful with me.

Not gentler exactly.

Less eager to prove a point.

When Clifford went inside, I said so.

"You argue differently with him."

"He has had enough people argue with his life."

The answer irritated me because it carried knowledge I did not have.

"You know what happened."

Holgrave looked toward the house.

"Some of it."

"And no one intends to tell me."

"That is not the same as no one intending you ever to know."

"It feels similar."

He did not answer.

The next morning Clifford asked me to bring the white roses again.

I did.

When I set them down, he touched one petal with the tip of his finger.

"Phoebe," he said.

"Yes?"

"You make the house less old."

I wanted to tell him that houses did not become younger.

Instead I said, "Only the rooms I can reach."

He smiled.

I did not understand then how many rooms remained beyond me.
